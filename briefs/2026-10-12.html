Monday, 12 October 2026

Washington went to Moscow for diesel, and the Gulf kept burning

Over the weekend Donald Trump waived sanctions so Russia can sell diesel again, then announced an “energy ceasefire” in Ukraine that Volodymyr Zelensky said was news to him and that Russia broke within hours. At the other end of the oil map, a Houthi missile attack on Riyadh’s main airport killed at least a dozen people, and three tankers struck mines in the Strait of Hormuz in two days. Elsewhere: Kosovo’s new war crimes law ran into Brussels, Raphaël Glucksmann took the French left’s nomination, Max Verstappen finally won in Singapore, and Serbia is thirteen days from voting.

Top Stories This Morning

01

Trump lifted sanctions on Russian diesel, then declared an energy ceasefire Russia broke the same day

On Friday Trump said Vladimir Putin had agreed to put more than 4 million tonnes of Russian diesel onto world markets — 300,000 tonnes at once, 500,000 in November, a million soon after — in exchange for a US Treasury general licence suspending sanctions on Russian fuel exports, reported as running to April 2027. On Sunday he posted that there was now an “ENERGY CEASEFIRE” between Russia and Ukraine and that both sides had agreed. Zelensky told Axios the announcement “was news for me,” Moscow has not confirmed any terms, and within hours Russian strikes hit the CHP-5 thermal plant in Kharkiv and another station near Kyiv. Zelensky counted more than 200 strikes on Ukrainian power generation and transmission and 17 on gas and oil sites in the past week, with over 150 people killed. He has offered to stop hitting Russian refineries if Russia stops hitting his grid; analysts reading the little that has been said suspect the truce, if it exists, covers refineries only. The diesel deal drew bipartisan criticism in Congress weeks after Trump signed fresh Russia sanctions.

Why it matters: American diesel and heating costs are the clearest threat to Trump’s party three weeks before the midterms, and the cheapest barrels available are Russian. That is the trade: sanctions relief for Moscow, paid for by Ukraine’s leverage over Russian refineries, with a ceasefire announcement attached that neither Kyiv nor the Kremlin has signed up to.

Sources: CNBC · NPR · Al Jazeera · Euromaidan Press · The Hill

02

A Houthi missile attack on Riyadh airport killed at least 12 and wounded about 300

Saudi authorities said a strike on King Khalid International Airport on Saturday killed at least 12 people and wounded roughly 300 — four Saudis among the dead, along with two Bangladeshis and one person each from the United States, Jordan, Palestine, Syria, Sudan and Egypt. It was the deadliest attack inside Saudi Arabia in more than a decade. The airport was struck again on Sunday, killing a Pakistani resident, injuring eleven and setting a fire in Terminal 4. The Houthis claimed the attacks as retaliation for Saudi strikes on Sanaa airport, bringing the toll from a week of attacks on Riyadh, Abha, Jazan and Najran to around 19 dead and more than 350 injured. Trump called Saturday’s strike “terrible” and said Washington is evaluating whether to join the Saudi-led offensive against the Houthis.

Why it matters: Three days after Trump promised not to strike Iran before 3 November, the question of American involvement has moved to Yemen, where no such promise applies. The Houthis have shown they can close the capital’s airport at will, which is both an argument for Saudi escalation and the reason Riyadh has spent years trying to get out of this war.

Sources: Al Jazeera · Al Jazeera · Bloomberg · Wikipedia summary of the attack series

03

Three tankers hit mines in the Strait of Hormuz in two days

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard navy said a supertanker capable of carrying about 2 million barrels struck a mine and caught fire in the strait on Sunday, the third vessel to hit one since Saturday. The IRGC said the ship was transiting an “unauthorised” route with its navigation and positioning systems switched off, and Iranian state media warned that any tanker ignoring the strait’s rules can expect to burn; the UAE rescued 22 crew from a tanker fire in the Gulf, though it is not confirmed to be the same ship. More than a dozen vessels have been attacked since late September. Tehran insists the strait is closed and under its “full control”; Secretary of State Marco Rubio says Iran has “lost complete control” of it and that the southern lane is safe. Talks are stalled: Iran’s security council secretary Mohsen Rezaei called the US reply to Iran’s seven conditions for reopening the waterway, passed through Qatari intermediaries, “insufficient.” Washington sanctioned 17 more shadow-fleet vessels.

Why it matters: Mines are the cheapest way to keep a strait shut without firing a shot, and they do not require Iran to win any engagement with the US Navy — only to make insurers nervous. That is why Brent is still above $100 while both governments claim the route is open.

Sources: Bernama / Anadolu · Middle East Eye · CBS News · Israel National News

Serbia & the Balkans

04

Thirteen days to Serbia’s vote: the roll is closed, the ballots go to print, and nobody agrees who is ahead

The electoral commission begins printing ballots today for the 25 October parliamentary election, after the ministry of public administration closed the unified voter register and the commission confirmed sixteen lists — one, “People’s Choice,” withdrew after endorsing the student list. The polling is irreconcilable. Faktor Plus, widely seen as close to the government, has Aleksandar Vučić’s United Serbia list at 48.6% against 37.1% for the student list, itself a 5.6-point jump in a month; the independent monitor CRTA had the students ahead, 44.9% to 35.7%, in the summer, and the pollster Vukadinović has them at 44.4% to 41.7%. Vučić, who resigned the presidency on 27 September to run for prime minister, told a rally in Zrenjanin he wants to win by half a million votes and asked why supporters thought Putin had said he hoped Vučić would get the job. Student activists say they have knocked on 170,000 doors in twenty days. CRTA declined to take part in an RTS debate, and the broadcast monitor BIRODI is tracking six channels.

Why it matters: Two sets of numbers describe two different elections — a comfortable government win, or something close enough to turn on turnout and the diaspora polling stations that did not open. Whoever loses on 25 October will have had a poll in hand all month saying they should have won.

Sources: N1 · Vreme · bne IntelliNews · European Western Balkans

05

Brussels says Kosovo’s new war crimes law breaks its commitments, and the new president has to sign it

The European Commission said the amendments Kosovo’s assembly passed on 6 October to the law governing the Hague-based Specialist Chambers are contrary to Kosovo’s international obligations to the EU, with spokesman Christian Wigand calling on Pristina to guarantee the court’s judicial independence and the safety of its witnesses. Brussels waited until the text was final before reacting, as it does with laws still in reading. The amendments were the price of Kosovo ending nearly two years of deadlock: opposition MPs agreed to elect a president only after Albin Kurti’s government let the bill through, and Justina Shiroka Pula, elected minutes before the midnight deadline on 6 October, must now endorse the law for it to bind. Protesters have filled Pristina’s main square since the Chambers convicted four Kosovo Liberation Army commanders of war crimes last week. Serbian politicians argue the changes have no legal effect on proceedings already under way in The Hague.

Why it matters: The court was created at the EU’s insistence and is written into Kosovo’s obligations to Brussels, so curbing it costs Pristina exactly the leverage it needs on visa liberalisation and accession. The new president’s first act is choosing between the deal that put her in office and the relationship that underwrites the state.

Sources: Euronews Albania · RFE/RL · Kosovo Online · Balkan Insight

World

06

Two weekend votes in Europe: Babiš’s party stumbles in the Czech Senate, and the French left picks Glucksmann

In the first round of the Czech Senate election on 9 and 10 October, Andrej Babiš’s ANO won none of the five seats decided outright, a year after returning to power; 22 of the 27 contested seats go to run-offs on 16 and 17 October, where thirteen ANO candidates each face a single opposition challenger, many from pro-Western alliances. Simultaneous municipal elections saw ANO take six regional capitals, two fewer than in 2022, and not Prague. In France, the MEP Raphaël Glucksmann won the Socialist-led primary on Sunday with about 66% of 120,787 votes, ahead of party leader Olivier Faure on 14.5% and Ségolène Royal on roughly 7%, making him the social-democratic candidate for the presidential first round on 18 April 2027. Voting had been extended by a day after a cyberattack disrupted its start. Polls currently put him around 10–13%, behind Marine Le Pen and Jordan Bardella.

Why it matters: The Czech Senate cannot bring down Babiš’s government, but it can block constitutional change and it is the first national read on him since he took office. Glucksmann’s win settles who speaks for the non-Mélenchon left in France — a bloc currently polling at a third of the far right.

Sources: Euronews · AP via ClickOrlando · Ara · Connexion France

07

The Nobel Peace Prize went to Navi Pillay

The Norwegian Nobel Committee awarded Friday’s prize to the South African jurist Navanethem “Navi” Pillay “for her efforts to promote peace and international law.” Pillay defended anti-apartheid detainees in Durban, became the first woman to open a law practice in her province, judged at the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda — where she sat on the first conviction for genocide — served as UN High Commissioner for Human Rights from 2008 to 2014, and most recently chaired the UN commission of inquiry that concluded Israel had committed genocide in Gaza. She is the fifth South African to win the prize. The ceremony is on 10 December in Oslo.

Why it matters: The committee answered the Ukraine-Gaza-Sudan question it was expected to answer by honouring the machinery of international law instead of any one war — and by choosing the author of the Gaza genocide finding, it took a position anyway.

Sources: The Nobel Prize · UN News · Al Jazeera

Business, Economy & Markets

08

Friday’s session: everything rallied once Washington said it would not hit Iran — now the inflation print

Equities recovered the week’s losses on Friday. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 423.31 points, or 0.83%, to 51,654.95; the S&P 500 added 0.59% to 7,811.54, back within reach of Monday’s record; the Nasdaq Composite gained 0.64% to 27,366.17 as chipmakers bounced from Thursday’s sell-off over OpenAI’s revenue restatement. Europe rose too — the STOXX 600 up 0.97% to 631.55, the DAX up 1.13% to 25,087 — though on the week the STOXX was flat and the DAX fell 0.57%. The common driver was relief rather than growth: Trump’s pledge not to strike Iran before 3 November steadied oil, with Brent settling at about $104.72, and stopped the bond market’s slide, the 10-year Treasury yield holding near 5.24% after touching its highest since 2002 midweek. Gold sat near $4,190–4,215 an ounce and bitcoin near $83,000. This week matters more than last: the bond market is shut today for Columbus Day while stocks trade, JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Citigroup and Wells Fargo open earnings season on Tuesday, and September CPI lands Wednesday with headline inflation expected to rise to 3.6% from 3.4%.

Why it matters: The Federal Reserve raised rates in September to 3.75–4.00%, which is why a 5.24% long bond and an energy shock are the same story. A hot core reading on Wednesday revives the case for another hike; a soft one lets the equity market keep pricing the diesel deal as disinflation.

Technology

09

Nvidia weighs buying the lab it already funds, as the FTC prepares formal demands on AI safety

Reports on Saturday said Nvidia is in early talks over a deeper tie-up with Reflection AI, the open-model startup it backed with $800 million and which raised at a $25 billion valuation earlier this year. Options under discussion range from an outright acquisition to an arrangement to hire its researchers and license its technology, or simply to sell it more compute; nothing is agreed and the talks may collapse. The stated rationale is a credible American answer to Chinese open-weight models. Separately, the Federal Trade Commission’s inquiry into frontier AI developers is moving from informal to formal: Semafor reports the agency is close to issuing investigative demands dozens of questions long to companies including OpenAI and Anthropic, and to the evaluation body METR, examining whether their safety claims amount to unfair or deceptive practices. A senior official said the agency is not yet ready to tell developers to stop doing anything. Trump has said referrals for criminal investigation are possible where rogue models threaten critical infrastructure.

Why it matters: Nvidia increasingly funds, backstops and now may own the customers whose demand justifies its own valuation, which is the circularity investors were already uneasy about after Thursday’s OpenAI revenue correction. The FTC’s demands would be the first time American regulators force the labs to document, under subpoena, the safety claims they make in public.

Science

10

A gene therapy gave ten blind patients some visual function back — using the technique that just won the Nobel

The New England Journal of Medicine published on 7 October an open-label trial of optogenetic therapy in ten people with advanced retinitis pigmentosa, a disease that destroys the light-sensing cells of the retina. The treatment delivers a gene for a light-sensitive protein to surviving retinal cells that are not normally photoreceptors, making them respond to light directly; patients recovered aspects of visual function. The authors are explicit that this is a safety study and that more research is needed on both safety and efficacy. It was published two days after the Nobel Assembly gave this year’s medicine prize to the development of optogenetics, work from the 2000s built to let neuroscientists switch individual neurons on and off with light.

Why it matters: Ten patients in an uncontrolled trial is the beginning of a therapy, not a cure, and nobody should expect this at a clinic soon. But it is the clearest demonstration yet that optogenetics does something outside a laboratory animal, which is the part of the Nobel citation that had been a promise rather than a result.

Sources: New England Journal of Medicine · The Nobel Prize

Automotive

11

The Paris Motor Show opens today, with Macron asking Europe to buy European

Emmanuel Macron opens the Mondial de l’Auto at Porte de Versailles today, attending as head of state for the last time, with stops at Stellantis, Renault and Valeo and a closing roundtable on autonomous driving. His argument is “European preference”: that public subsidies for electric cars should favour vehicles built inside the EU across the whole value chain, from batteries to spare parts, and not reward “screwdriver factories” that do only final assembly here. Handelsblatt reported a Paris–Berlin understanding on CO2 targets and made-in-Europe rules; the Élysée denied any agreement exists while confirming talks continue. France has 400,000 direct jobs in the industry and is far from its target of two million EVs built a year by 2030. The show runs to 18 October and hosts a long list of Chinese brands, none of whose executives were at Sunday’s Élysée dinner. The backdrop is a record September: battery-electric cars took about 33% of new registrations across twelve major European markets, up from 22% a year earlier, and 34.5% in Germany.

Why it matters: Europe’s EV demand problem is solving itself; its industrial problem is not, because a growing share of those sales is Chinese-built. Macron is proposing to answer with content rules rather than tariffs, days before Brussels revisits the 2035 combustion-engine deadline on 23 November.
