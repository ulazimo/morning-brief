Top Stories This Morning

01

The Houthis take Perim Island, and the second oil chokepoint changes hands

Yemen's Houthis seized the Red Sea port of Mokha on Thursday and reached Perim Island, the rocky outcrop that splits the Bab el-Mandeb Strait into its two shipping lanes, on Friday — giving them effective command of the passage that carries trade between Asia and Europe. Traffic had already collapsed: commercial tracking put transits at 29–30 ships a day at the start of last week and 12 by Thursday, with lines rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope. Separately, drones launched from Iraq struck Saudi Arabia's East–West pipeline, and Riyadh shut it as a precaution with no restart date announced. Saudi Arabia and the Houthis traded strikes over the weekend, and Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman twice asked Donald Trump to hit the group; the administration has so far declined.

Why it matters: The East–West pipeline is the route Saudi Arabia uses to move crude to the Red Sea when the Strait of Hormuz is unusable — so its closure, together with Bab el-Mandeb, removes the workaround and the alternative at the same time.

Sources: NPR · NBC News · CBC · CBS News

02

Oman postpones today's Hormuz talks, and Brent runs back toward $108

Iran and the Gulf states were due to meet in Salalah this morning to negotiate a temporary shipping corridor through the Strait of Hormuz. Omani Foreign Minister Badr Albusaidi said on Sunday the meeting had been put off "in the interests of consensus"; no regional government had confirmed attendance and Bahrain had ruled it out. Friday's session had run the other way — Brent settled 2.8% lower at $104.61 on the first news that the talks were on, closing out a week up nearly 9%. This morning Brent traded back toward $108 and WTI near $103, a four-month high.

Why it matters: Diplomacy was the only thing pricing oil below its wartime highs, and the Federal Reserve begins a two-day meeting tomorrow with an energy shock it cannot influence now widening rather than easing.

Sources: Arab News · Bloomberg · Trading Economics

03

Sweden's centre-left wins by three seats, and the far right is shut out of government

Magdalena Andersson's Social Democrats took 28.1% in Sunday's general election against 19.9% for Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson's Moderates, and the preliminary count gives the centre-left bloc 176 seats to 173 for the governing right-wing alliance. Kristersson had promised the Sweden Democrats cabinet posts — the first far-right ministers in Swedish history — if his bloc held. Counting of late postal ballots and votes cast at embassies abroad begins on Wednesday, and the final allocation is not expected before the end of the week; on a margin this thin, the outcome is not yet formally settled.

Why it matters: After a run of hard-right advances across Europe, Sweden has kept the Sweden Democrats out of office — by three seats, and subject to a count that is not finished.

Sources: Al Jazeera · Euronews · The Irish Times

Serbia & the Balkans

04

The ruling party files first; the students file this afternoon

Darko Glišić submitted the list "Aleksandar Vučić — Ujedinjena Srbija" to the Republic Electoral Commission on Saturday with 250 candidates and 224,557 signatures, the first list in for the 25 October election; the Socialists followed the same day, and the commission has since proclaimed the governing list. The "Studentska lista — Studenti pobeđuju" spent the weekend collecting signatures to long queues in Belgrade, Novi Sad, Niš, Kragujevac and elsewhere, and will submit this afternoon — candidate Željko Hubač says the full slate of names becomes public only at that moment. The third anti-government column, "Evropska Srbija — Marinika Tepić i Zdravko Ponoš", is still gathering.

Why it matters: Signature counts are the only hard numbers either side has before polling, and today is the first time voters see who is actually on the students' list rather than the movement behind it.

Sources: Danas · KoSSev · N1

05

Kosovo waits for the Thaçi verdict on Wednesday

The Kosovo Specialist Chambers in The Hague will deliver its first-instance judgment against former president Hashim Thaçi, Kadri Veseli, Rexhep Selimi and Jakup Krasniqi at 10:00 CET on Wednesday. The four former KLA leaders are charged with war crimes and crimes against humanity against Serbs, Roma and Albanians regarded as collaborators during the 1998–99 war; prosecutors asked for 45 years each in February. Evidence closed in December 2025 after a trial that opened in April 2023, and 156 victims participated. Prosecutors are separately seeking six years for Thaçi in a linked obstruction-of-justice case. Rallies in support of the four were held in Kosovo over the weekend.

Why it matters: The verdict lands five weeks before Serbia's election and will be read in both capitals as a judgment on the war itself, not only on four men.

Sources: Kosovo Specialist Chambers · European Western Balkans · Eurasia Review

World

06

France investigates sabotage after a Normandy derailment injures 44

A regional train carrying 186 passengers from Rouen to Caen derailed at Cléon, in Seine-Maritime, on Friday evening, injuring 44 people; one 18-year-old woman was airlifted to Rouen University Hospital and is no longer in danger. Investigators found a detached piece of rail on the track, which national police said was "probably" linked to the derailment, and are working to establish whether the cause was accidental or deliberate. No arrests have been announced.

Why it matters: France has treated rail sabotage as a live security problem since the coordinated attacks on the TGV network before the 2024 Olympics; a confirmed second case would move this from an accident inquiry to a national-security one.

Sources: France 24 · NBC News

07

Russia fires roughly 500 drones in a day as the next talks slip to October

Russia launched close to 500 strike drones at Ukraine over the course of Saturday, in a barrage that reached Volyn and Rivne in the far west. Volodymyr Zelenskyy, responding to proposals circulating in Washington to ease sanctions on Russian businessmen as a confidence-building step, said "500 Shahed drones per day is certainly not the right time to even begin a discussion about easing sanctions." Ukrainian military intelligence chief Kyrylo Budanov said the next round of US–Ukraine–Russia talks may not happen until October; Sergei Lavrov has again refused a ceasefire during negotiations or a meeting at leader level.

Why it matters: The American push to restart negotiations is now being measured against a Russian tempo that has risen rather than paused, which is the argument sanctions-relief sceptics in Europe have been making all year.

Sources: The Kyiv Independent · PBS NewsHour

Business, Economy & Markets

08

Friday's session: a hot CPI, and shares rallied anyway

US consumer prices rose 0.4% in August and held at 3.4% year on year, in line with forecasts, but core CPI rose 0.3% on the month — a tenth above expectations — with the annual core rate at 2.4%. Energy did the damage again: fuel costs were up 28% on the year and diesel 52%. Traders read it as confirmation and pushed the odds of a 25bp Fed hike on Wednesday to roughly 85–90%, from about 60% earlier in the week.

Equities rallied regardless, snapping a four-day losing run as oil eased on the Hormuz headlines. The Dow gained 509.19 points, or 0.98%, to 52,573.29; the S&P 500 rose 0.86% to 7,656.98; the Nasdaq Composite added 0.96% to 26,333.04. Europe was quieter — the STOXX 600 rose 0.3% and the DAX ended slightly higher, led by banks (Santander +1.3%, BBVA +1.0%) and aerospace (Safran +2.0%, Airbus +1.6%). The 10-year Treasury finished at about 4.96%, still the highest since 2023. Gold held near $4,385 an ounce and bitcoin traded around $77,300.

Why it matters: A hike this week is now close to fully priced, which means the market risk on Wednesday is not the decision but Kevin Warsh's guidance on whether one increase is the end of it.

Sources: CNBC · CBS News · The Washington Post · ETF Trends · USAGOLD

Technology

09

Anthropic's chief executive calls for slowing down, and his rivals agree in public

Dario Amodei published an essay on Saturday, "We Must Pace the Frontier", arguing that the industry should deliberately slow the rate at which model capabilities improve so that alignment work, security and outside evaluation can keep up. He gives two reasons for changing his mind: recursive self-improvement, with models now materially helping build the next generation, and an agent-swarm incident he treats as a warning that misaligned systems could do serious cyber damage within 6–12 months. The plan has three steps — embedded third-party evaluators, then industry coordination, then global coordination — and Anthropic is committing unilaterally to the first, giving evaluators such as METR permanent, employee-level access to systems and training pipelines. Sam Altman said OpenAI would match the commitment; Elon Musk posted that "Dario is right."

Why it matters: Frontier labs have competed on release speed for three years; a voluntary pledge to let outsiders inside the training process is the first concrete safety commitment the largest of them have all endorsed.

Sources: Dario Amodei · TechCrunch · Unite.AI

Science

10

A 400-year link between the Pacific and Indian oceans has come apart

A study published in Nature Communications reconstructs tropical climate back to the early 1600s using corals, tree rings and stalagmites, and finds that the Indian Ocean's basin-wide temperature pattern has tracked the Pacific Walker circulation reliably for four centuries — broken only briefly by major 19th-century volcanic eruptions. In recent decades the relationship has not merely weakened but reversed sign. The authors attribute it to greenhouse warming: the tropical Indian Ocean has warmed about 0.1°C per decade since the 1950s while the Pacific Walker circulation has strengthened since the 1980s.

Why it matters: Seasonal forecasts for monsoon Asia and East Africa lean on the Pacific to predict the Indian Ocean; if that link no longer holds, the forecasts several billion people plant and plan around lose one of their anchors.

Sources: Nature Communications · ScienceDaily

11

Stellantis moves to sell a car plant to an armoured-vehicle maker

Stellantis has signed a memorandum of understanding with Roshel, a Canadian armoured-vehicle manufacturer, on the potential sale of its idled assembly plant in Brampton, Ontario. The site was being retooled for Jeep production when the company paused the plan in early 2025 and then moved Jeep Compass output to the United States, leaving it empty. Roshel wants it for a defence-manufacturing centre. Unifor, the union representing Stellantis workers in Canada, has paused contract talks over the plant's future. In the same week, suppliers were told the extended-range Jeep Grand Wagoneer slips to November 2027 and the Ram 1500 REV to around April 2027 — six and nine months late respectively.

Why it matters: A car plant converted to defence work is the clearest single illustration of where European and North American industrial capital is actually going as electrification timelines slide.

Sources: CBC · CP24 · Electrek
