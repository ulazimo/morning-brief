The Gulf War Reaches Saudi Soil, and Brent Reaches for $100

Top Stories This Morning

01

Houthi drones and missiles hit Saudi energy sites; 73 wounded

A wave of Iran-aligned Houthi attacks on Tuesday struck the Saudi cities of Abha, Khamis Mushait, Jazan and Najran, igniting fires at oil facilities and utilities and wounding 73 people, including women and children. Operations at several sites were temporarily suspended, among them the 400,000 barrel-a-day Jazan refinery, one of the kingdom's largest. Riyadh has vowed to retaliate.

Why it matters: Until now the war has been fought between Washington and Tehran at sea; hitting Saudi refining capacity pulls the region's largest oil producer directly into it and puts physical supply, not just shipping risk, on the table.

Sources: NPR · NBC News · Reuters via US News

02

Canada's counter-tariffs take effect as the North American trade war escalates

Canadian duties of 15%, 25% and 50% on roughly C$27.6bn of US imports came into force on 8 September, covering steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper and electronics. They answer US tariffs of up to 50% imposed under Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act — a provision unused since 1949 — after trade talks between Ottawa and Washington collapsed in late August.

Why it matters: This is now a two-way tariff war between the US and its largest trading partner, with cost increases landing on manufacturers on both sides of the border just as inflation data has become the deciding input for the Federal Reserve.

Sources: CNBC · Blakes tariff timeline

03

Serbia's government formally asks Vučić to dissolve parliament

Prime Minister Đuro Macut's cabinet decided on Monday to request dissolution of the National Assembly, starting the clock on a snap election. Under Serbian rules the president must issue the decree — or publicly decline — within 72 hours, and the vote must be called by 10 September to be held on 25 October, the date Vučić has signalled. He has said he will resign the presidency to campaign, with his party proposing him as its candidate for prime minister.

Why it matters: After nearly two years of anti-corruption protests triggered by the Novi Sad canopy collapse, Vučić is choosing the ground and the timing for the first national test of his rule — and, if it works, moving his power base from the presidency to the office that actually runs the government.

Sources: Al Jazeera · European Western Balkans · AP via Washington Post

Serbia & the Balkans

04

Bosnia heads to a general election on 4 October without Dodik on the ballot

Campaigning is under way for Bosnia and Herzegovina's general election, the first since Milorad Dodik was stripped of the Republika Srpska presidency and banned from office for six years for defying the international High Representative. Dodik still leads the SNSD, whose Siniša Karan won February's rerun and holds the post until October; the main Serb opposition parties have agreed to run behind SDS leader Branko Blanuša.

Why it matters: The RS presidency and the Bosniak-Croat-Serb tripartite presidency together decide whether Bosnia spends the next four years inching toward EU accession or relitigating secession.

Sources: European Western Balkans · Balkan Insight

World

05

Merz doubles down after the AfD landslide, and rules out any deal in Magdeburg

Two days after the AfD took 43.8% in Saxony-Anhalt against 17.2% for his own CDU, Chancellor Friedrich Merz used a CDU leadership meeting to tell allies that Germany's "institutions are resilient and stable" and to insist he will push through his unpopular economic reforms rather than soften them. No party will govern with the AfD, which Germany's domestic intelligence service classifies as right-wing extremist, so a coalition in Magdeburg still has to be assembled from the parties that lost.

Why it matters: It is the first time a far-right party has won a German state election since 1945, and the arithmetic — a plurality that cannot govern — is the template for the next federal contest.

Sources: Fortune · Al Jazeera

06

Russia strikes Kyiv the day after the US envoys leave; five killed

Missiles and drones hit the Ukrainian capital early on Tuesday, killing five people, hours after the three-day halt on attacks on both capitals expired at the end of 8 September. Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner had met Vladimir Putin in Moscow and Volodymyr Zelenskyy in Kyiv, securing agreement to resume trilateral talks but no ceasefire; Moscow rejected an air truce and repeated its demand that Ukraine withdraw from the rest of Donbas.

Why it matters: Three rounds of US shuttle diplomacy this year have produced short pauses and no change in Russia's terms, which are still the ones Kyiv cannot accept.

Sources: CNN · Ukrainska Pravda · Kyiv Independent

07

The tanker war continues: US strikes near Kharg and Jask, Iran readies an "exclusion zone"

US Central Command struck Iranian tankers and infrastructure near the export terminals at Kharg Island and Jask on 8 September, in response to attempted Iranian missile attacks on US warships; Iranian agencies said four missiles hit a tanker about 11km offshore with no casualties. Tehran's Supreme National Security Council says it will shortly declare an exclusion zone running from the US naval blockade line to the Strait of Hormuz, blacklisting any vessel that approaches without coordinating with Iran.

Why it matters: Kharg handles up to 90% of Iran's crude exports, and a blacklist that reaches insurers rather than only navies would thin traffic through Hormuz without a shot being fired.

Sources: Reuters via Yahoo · PBS NewsHour · Lloyd's List

Business, Economy & Markets

08

Tuesday's session: oil does the damage, and a Fed hike is now the base case

Wall Street fell for a second day. The Dow dropped 1.2%, or 628 points, to 52,786; the S&P 500 lost 0.6% to 7,673; the Nasdaq Composite slipped 0.3% to 26,421, cushioned by AI-infrastructure names. The driver was crude: Brent rose about 2.2% to $99.16 and WTI 3.3% to $94.46 after the Houthi strikes on Saudi facilities, reviving inflation fears just as Canada's tariffs took effect. Europe barely moved — the STOXX 600 closed flat at 649.6 — but Novartis fell roughly 14%, its worst day on record, after a disappointing readout on its myotonic dystrophy therapy, and Boston Scientific dropped 5.9% after saying a summer cyber-incident would cost it its 2026 guidance. The 10-year Treasury yield held near 4.77%, close to three-year highs; gold eased around $4,400 an ounce.

Why it matters: With August payrolls at 162,000 against a forecast of 56,000, traders now put roughly 60% odds on a 25bp hike at the FOMC meeting on 15–16 September — an unusual position for a market to be in, and Friday's CPI is the last data point before it.

Sources: Kiplinger · Reuters via Business Recorder · Trading Economics

Technology

09

China sets out a five-year plan to more than quadruple its AI compute

The Ministry of Industry and Information Technology published a 2026–2030 blueprint on 8 September targeting 9,800 exaflops of national AI computing capacity by 2030, up from about 2,450 exaflops in July, backed by ¥3.8tn (roughly $532bn) of cumulative IT infrastructure spending. It calls for the orderly build-out of clusters of 10,000 and 100,000 accelerator cards on top of the existing "East Data, West Computing" hub network, where 52 ten-thousand-card facilities are already running.

Why it matters: It is an explicit plan to reach frontier-scale compute using domestic accelerators, which is the part US export controls were designed to prevent.

Sources: South China Morning Post · DigiTimes

10

Claude produces a machine-checked proof of Fermat's Last Theorem

Anthropic said on 5 September that its model had completed the first end-to-end, computer-verified Lean 4 formalisation of Fermat's Last Theorem, working largely autonomously over 11 days. The run produced about 13 million lines of Lean and proved 30,300 theorems; it used Prove2Me, an open-source harness from a Columbia University group, added mid-run after a first attempt failed. Wiles's 1995 proof runs to 129 pages and took human referees months to check; formalising it was expected to take years.

Why it matters: Formal verification is one of the few domains where an AI system's output can be checked mechanically rather than trusted, which makes this a measurable data point on autonomous long-horizon work rather than a benchmark score.

Sources: Nature · Anthropic

Science

11

Physicists measure gravity's effect on a falling quantum object for the first time

A team led by Ben-Gurion University with Ulm, Oxford, Southampton, the German Aerospace Center and Texas A&M — and including Roger Penrose — reported in Science Advances that it had directly measured the quantum phase acquired by atoms in free fall. The measured effect matches what Einstein's equivalence principle predicts when applied to a quantum object.

Why it matters: It is a clean experimental handhold where general relativity and quantum mechanics meet — not a theory of quantum gravity, but the first direct measurement of a prediction that sits on the boundary between them.

Sources: University of Oxford · ScienceDaily

Automotive

12

Jaguar Land Rover cuts 4,000 jobs, and Britain rules out a bailout

JLR said on Monday it will cut about 4,000 roles — roughly a tenth of its global workforce, mostly non-production and mostly in the UK — over two years, targeting £1.7bn of savings to fund £15–18bn of investment in electrification and software over five years. The Tata Motors-owned carmaker posted a £244m loss for the year to March, against a £1.8bn profit the year before, after a cyberattack halted production for over a month at a cost of £260m. Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds ruled out state support.

Why it matters: Coming days after Volkswagen approved another 50,000 cuts, it marks the same squeeze — Chinese EV competition plus an expensive electric transition — reaching the premium end of the European industry.

Sources: CNBC · Bloomberg · ITV News
