Top Stories This Morning

01

Alberta votes to stay in Canada, by about three to one

With roughly 86% of polling locations reporting, about 76% of Albertans chose to remain part of Canada against 24% who wanted the province to begin the legal process toward a binding referendum on secession. Premier Danielle Smith, who called the vote but opposed separation, and Prime Minister Mark Carney both welcomed the result; separatist organisers said they had turned a fringe cause into a movement that will not disappear. The result is non-binding, and counts remain preliminary.

Why it matters: A 24% separatist vote is both smaller than the movement claimed and larger than any previous Alberta benchmark, so it becomes the number Smith cites in the next fight with Ottawa over pipelines and emissions rules — and the reason Carney can now treat national unity as settled rather than pending.

Sources: CTV News · Al Jazeera · AP via PBS · Axios

02

Tesla and SpaceX form committees to study a merger, with Musk shut out of both

On SpaceX’s third-quarter earnings call on Tuesday, Elon Musk confirmed that the independent directors of each board have formed special committees to evaluate combining the two companies. He said he is a conflicted party under securities rules and is therefore not a member of either committee and not involved in the talks. No agreement has been reached, he said, and anyone treating a deal as a certainty “would be overstating it.” Any transaction would need the approval of both shareholder bases, including holders of 50% of Tesla’s outstanding shares. Musk argued the case on AI software, chip design, robotics and connectivity; speculation has built since SpaceX acquired xAI in February and went public in June.

Why it matters: This would be the largest corporate combination ever attempted — Fortune puts the pair near $3.5 trillion — and it would fold a listed carmaker’s public shareholders into a launch, satellite and defence contractor, which is why the process has been handed to independent directors rather than the man who controls both.

Sources: NBC News · Axios · Fortune · Investing.com

03

Pegasus found on the Belgrade rector’s phone, four days before Serbia votes

Citizen Lab, working with Belgrade’s SHARE Foundation, said on Monday that it had established with high confidence that the iPhone of Vladan Đokić, rector of the University of Belgrade and one of the most visible figures in the student-led protest movement, was infected this year with NSO Group’s Pegasus spyware through a zero-click exploit that required no action by him. A second target, former prosecutor Rade Bajić, received an Apple threat notification; neither he nor the researchers could confirm an infection. The report does not name who was behind the attacks and says the investigation is continuing. Citizen Lab now counts at least 16 members of Serbian civil society publicly known to have been targeted or infected with mercenary spyware. The BIA, Serbia’s civilian security service, dismissed a similar set of allegations in September.

Why it matters: Pegasus is sold only to states, so a confirmed infection of the rector’s phone during an election campaign he helped set in motion puts a government agency in the frame days before a vote whose legitimacy is already the central dispute.

Sources: Citizen Lab · AP via Rappler · The Hacker News · Serbian Monitor

Serbia & the Balkans

04

Serbia’s last polls disagree on who is ahead, and Belgrade is blaming Zagreb

An NSPM survey published on Tuesday put the student-backed Students Win list at 44.5% among decided voters against 42.7% for the coalition around Aleksandar Vučić’s Progressive Party — the first poll in weeks to show the students in front. The pro-government pollster Faktor Plus, surveying a month earlier, had the ruling list at 48.6% to 37.1%. PolitPro’s weighted average still gives the Progressives a narrow lead. Roughly a quarter of voters tell pollsters they have not decided, and the smaller lists — the Socialists, European Serbia, Authentic Right — are clustered around the 3% threshold.

Serbia’s foreign ministry has sent Croatia a protest note accusing it of interfering in the campaign, singling out Croatian media coverage. Prime Minister Andrej Plenković called the accusation “complete nonsense” and political spin that casts Croatia as a “half-external and half-internal enemy,” adding that “our media are free — people write what they want.” Vučić said no country had interfered in a Serbian election as brutally as Croatia.

Why it matters: Two credible pollsters a week apart disagree by eleven points, which means Sunday’s count will be contested rather than conceded — and an undecided quarter of the electorate is large enough to make the turnout machine, not the polling, decisive.

Sources: bne IntelliNews · Polling table · Vreme · BTA · ECFR on the scenarios

05

Kosovo, Serbia and Bosnia are all at risk of losing EU growth-plan money

The European Commission has warned that slow implementation of agreed reforms puts Kosovo, Serbia and Bosnia and Herzegovina at risk of forfeiting hundreds of millions of euros between them under the Western Balkans growth plan, which pays out against delivered benchmarks rather than promises. In Kosovo’s case the delay is traceable to the institutional deadlock that has consumed most of this year; the prime minister’s office says substantial reform agendas have now been adopted. Separately, Washington’s decision to lift sanctions on the Bosnian Serb leader Milorad Dodik has left him free to deepen ties with Moscow without restriction.

Why it matters: The growth plan is the EU’s main remaining lever in the region now that accession itself has stalled — if money intended as an incentive goes unpaid because of domestic politics, Brussels loses the lever and the governments lose the funds.

Sources: Balkan Insight · Ground News (regional roundup)

World

06

Two more tankers burn in Hormuz, and the IMO says it cannot guarantee safe passage

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard said on Tuesday that mines it had laid set two “violating” oil tankers ablaze and stopped them, and a later statement claimed a third vessel — reportedly carrying more than two million barrels and running with its navigation equipment switched off — also struck a mine. Iran’s Persian Gulf Strait Authority has added 122 ships to a list of tankers it accuses of breaching its transit rules. The secretary-general of the International Maritime Organization said plainly that “we cannot guarantee that a vessel that intends to transit that route will not be hit by a mine.” President Masoud Pezeshkian said Washington must drop its pressure, threats and sanctions before any negotiation; indirect contacts via Oman, Qatar and Pakistan continue without a breakthrough. Iranian outlets also claimed another US MQ-9 drone was shot down over the strait, which has not been independently confirmed.

Why it matters: A named international regulator saying it cannot vouch for safe transit is what converts a shipping risk into a marine-insurance fact, and it is the step that keeps tankers out of the strait even on days when nothing is hit.

Sources: Reuters via FMT · CBS News · Herald Business · Benzinga

07

Ebola reaches Nigeria as Kenya’s cluster grows to nine

Nigeria confirmed its first case of the current outbreak on Tuesday: a woman who arrived from Uganda and was treated for several days at a private hospital in Abuja before laboratory confirmation. The federal government said its presidential task force and the NCDC would issue updates. Kenya, meanwhile, confirmed a ninth case — a 24-year-old man who was already in quarantine as a contact of the index patient and tested positive on 19 October. Health director-general Patrick Amoth said all nine Kenyan infections belong to a single known cluster in Nairobi with no evidence of community transmission, and that 128 of 130 identified contacts have been traced. The epidemic is centred on the Democratic Republic of Congo, involves the Bundibugyo strain for which no vaccine is licensed, and was declared a public health emergency of international concern by the WHO in May.

Why it matters: Nigeria is Africa’s most populous country and Abuja is a major air hub, so a case detected only after days in a private hospital tests exactly the weak point — time to diagnosis — that turned the 2014 West African outbreak into an epidemic.

Sources: AP via ABC News · NPR · Washington Post · CDC situation summary

08

Trump says it is time for someone other than Zelensky to negotiate for Ukraine

Speaking at the White House on Monday, Donald Trump said “Zelenskyy doesn’t know what he’s talking about” and that “it’s time for somebody else to negotiate on behalf of Ukraine,” while repeating that he is open to meeting Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping — a session he has framed around nuclear arms reduction rather than a Ukraine settlement. The remarks followed his executive order waiving most of the new tariffs and sanctions due to take effect under the Graham Act, replacing them with a review of up to 60 days. Zelensky called the waiver “wrong” and said it helps only Russia; Friedrich Merz agreed it was the wrong decision; Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer called it a “deal with the devil,” and several Republicans objected publicly. Russia’s Kirill Dmitriev called the suspension historic. Finland, meanwhile, said it would buy a million anti-personnel mines for more than $100 million.

Why it matters: The American president is now publicly questioning the standing of the counterpart he would have to sign any deal with, which pushes Europe toward underwriting Ukraine’s position itself — the same calculation behind Helsinki ordering mines.

Sources: RFE/RL · CNBC · CBS News · Democracy Now headlines

Business, Economy & Markets

09

The Dow adds 1% on earnings and cheaper oil; the AI trade sits it out again

Tuesday was a rotation, not a rally. The Dow rose 529.85 points, or 1.02%, to 52,495.88, led by 3M’s more than 5% gain after an earnings beat and a raised full-year outlook. The S&P 500 added 0.28% to 7,712.57 while the Nasdaq Composite finished flat, down 0.02% at 26,424.82, as megacap technology and chip-equipment names lagged for a fourth session. Europe was quiet: the STOXX 600 slipped 0.14% and the DAX 0.06%, to 25,304.42.

Oil did the work. WTI climbed toward $100 in the morning on the Hormuz mine strikes, then reversed sharply on conflicting reports about possible US–Iran contacts, with the December contract settling near $97.80 — the drop in energy costs being precisely what lifted the industrial end of the market. The ten-year Treasury yield eased two basis points to about 5.12%. Gold fell to roughly $4,142 an ounce despite the softer yields, with traders positioning for a Federal Reserve that is expected to hold on 28 October and may raise in December.

After the close, Netflix reported earnings of $0.87 a share against the $0.82 expected, on revenue of $12.87 billion, up about 12% year on year, with operating margin at 34.6%. The stock fell around 6% in extended trading anyway: the company cut its 2026 advertising revenue forecast to about $2.7 billion from roughly $3 billion, blaming a slower rollout of ad capabilities, and guided full-year revenue to the low end of its range at $51.1 billion.

Why it matters: With oil off its highs and real-economy earnings beating, money is moving out of the AI complex and into cyclicals — and Netflix’s punishment for a strong quarter with a weak ad number shows the market is now pricing the new revenue line, not the old one.

Sources: CNBC markets · Reuters via Investing.com · Kitco · CNBC on Netflix · Netflix 8-K

Technology

10

China’s big platforms order $35 billion of export-legal Nvidia cards — and will not get them

ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent have together ordered more than three million of Nvidia’s RTX PRO 5500 cards, the version engineered to stay inside US export limits, at roughly $11,400 each for a total above $35 billion, Reuters reported. Nvidia has told partners it can produce no more than three million of the compliant chips worldwide in 2027, and that customers should expect only 5–10% of what they asked for. Separately, Politico reported that OpenAI has stopped pressing Capitol Hill for tighter restrictions on advanced chip sales to China, a reversal that removes the most prominent corporate voice arguing for the controls and hands Nvidia a win in that fight.

Why it matters: Demand from three Chinese firms alone exceeds Nvidia’s entire planned supply of export-compliant parts, which tells you the controls are now rationing rather than blocking — and that the binding constraint on Chinese AI capacity is Nvidia’s factory allocation, not Washington’s rules.

Sources: Reuters reporting via Tech Startups · Politico reporting via TNW · Axios on in-house chips

Science

11

Model glaciers day by day instead of month by month, and they melt faster

A peer-reviewed study in Nature Communications by researchers at ETH Zurich and the Vrije Universiteit Brussel rebuilt the Global Glacier Evolution Model to run at daily rather than monthly resolution and applied it to more than 12,500 glaciers holding 94% of the world’s glacier ice. Resolving short heatwaves instead of averaging them away raises the projected glacier contribution to sea-level rise this century by 5–10%, or roughly 20 to 33 millimetres more by 2100. Lead author Lander Van Tricht said a heatwave lasting only a few days can melt enormous amounts of ice, and that monthly averaging systematically understates those extremes. This is a modelling result, not a new observation: the glaciers are not melting faster than before, but the models were underestimating how fast.

Why it matters: Every coastal defence plan is built on sea-level projections produced by models of exactly this kind, and a systematic 5–10% underestimate traceable to time resolution is the sort of correction that propagates into the next IPCC assessment.

Sources: Phys.org · VUB press release · Nature Communications
