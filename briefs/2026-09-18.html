One Energy Shock, Three Central Banks, Three Different Answers

Top Stories This Morning

01

The Fed hikes, the Bank of England refuses, and the Bank of Japan moves this morning

Three of the world's four big central banks decided within 40 hours of each other, all facing the same imported energy inflation, and all reached different conclusions. On Wednesday the Federal Reserve raised its target range a quarter-point to 3.75–4.00% in a unanimous 12–0 vote, its first increase in three years; 16 of the 18 dots in the new projections see at least one more rise this year, and officials marked up 2026 headline PCE inflation to 3.7% and core to 3.4%. On Thursday the Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% by 6–3, with three members voting for 4% after UK CPI hit a five-month high of 3.1% in August; the committee also voted unanimously to run its gilt holdings down to zero, at an average of about £46bn a year. Early this morning the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate by a quarter-point to 1.25%, a 31-year high, on a 7–2 vote, citing the risk that inflation overshoots its 2% target. The yen weakened about 0.45% to 156.6 per dollar on the decision and the 10-year JGB yield fell nearly 5bp to 2.95%.

Why it matters: The same shock — oil and gas prices lifted by the war around the Strait of Hormuz — is being read as a reason to tighten in Washington and Tokyo and as a reason to wait in London. That divergence is where currency moves come from, and a weaker yen and a softer pound make the imported energy that caused the problem more expensive again.

Sources: CNBC · Charles Schwab · Bank of England · Al Jazeera · CNBC

02

Thaçi gets 25 years, and Pristina takes to the streets

The Kosovo Specialist Chambers in The Hague convicted former president Hashim Thaçi and three other former Kosovo Liberation Army leaders of war crimes on Wednesday, sentencing Thaçi and Jakup Krasniqi to 25 years, Kadri Veseli to 18 and Rexhep Selimi to 13. The trial panel found them responsible for the arbitrary detention of 385 people, the torture of 303, cruel treatment of 49 and 96 murders in Kosovo and northern Albania in 1998–99, against Kosovo Albanians, Serbs, Roma and others; all four were acquitted of crimes against humanity. Prime Minister Albin Kurti called it "a great and harmful punishment" and said the findings were unjust and would be reviewed on appeal. Crowds marched on the government building and the EU rule-of-law mission EULEX in Pristina on Wednesday and again on Thursday, throwing stones and firecrackers and waving KLA flags. A Serbian minister said the ruling proved the KLA had been a criminal organisation; Human Rights Watch called the verdicts a step toward justice for victims.

Why it matters: This was the only senior-leadership war crimes trial running anywhere, and its conclusion reopens the founding argument of Kosovo's statehood five weeks before Serbia votes. Both governments will campaign on it, and the anger is being directed at the EU mission that Kosovo's own institutions depend on.

Sources: NPR · Balkan Insight · Euronews · Human Rights Watch

03

Brussels offers Canada a status that does not yet exist

In her State of the Union address in Strasbourg on Wednesday, with Prime Minister Mark Carney sitting in the chamber as her guest, European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen proposed opening the door for Canada to become the EU's first "associate member". Carney welcomed the offer on Thursday while conceding that the term "remains to be defined", and said there would be a debate and a vote in the Canadian Parliament on the future relationship: "we are at the start of a road." Ottawa has been looking for alternatives to Washington through a year of tariffs, demands for economic concessions and repeated talk from President Trump of annexing Canada. The same speech pledged up to €15bn of unspent EU defence loans for joint arms projects with Ukraine, plus winter energy and air-defence support — and, conspicuously, said nothing about what to do with Russia's frozen assets.

Why it matters: There is no associate membership in the EU treaties, so this is an invitation to invent one — and whatever Brussels builds for Canada becomes the template for everyone else standing outside the club, from the UK to the Western Balkans.

Sources: NPR · Washington Post · European Commission · Axios

Serbia & the Balkans

04

Vučić turns his fire on the student list as the campaign opens

President Aleksandar Vučić said on Thursday that the student movement had been pressuring opposition parties to sit out the 25 October snap parliamentary election, and dismissed its slate as one that "features neither workers, nor farmers, nor real students, save for those who orchestrated rebellions at the universities". The Student List, which registered 250 candidates and about 75,000 signatures on Monday, launched its campaign this week. Vučić repeated that he will resign the presidency on 25 or 26 September, once he returns from the United States, and campaign as a private citizen — a step the law does not require of him.

Why it matters: Serbia allocates seats by D'Hondt, which punishes a split opposition, so the question of whether the students and the established opposition parties run as one column or two decides the arithmetic. Both sides know it, which is why the pressure is being fought over in public.

Sources: N1 · N1 · AP via ABC News

World

05

A UN-mandated mission finds grounds to believe the US committed war crimes in Iran

The independent international fact-finding mission set up by the UN Human Rights Council said on Thursday it has "reasonable grounds" to believe the United States committed war crimes in two airstrikes that killed at least 178 civilians. One hit the Shajareh Tayyebeh school in Minab in February, at the start of the US–Israeli war on Iran; Iranian state media put the death toll there at 168, most of them children. The other struck a sports centre in Lamerd, killing 22 and injuring more than 100. The investigators concluded that the school building was the intended point of impact rather than collateral damage from a strike on an adjacent Revolutionary Guard compound.

Why it matters: The findings are not binding and no court has jurisdiction over the US here, but they become part of the evidentiary record that outlives the war — and they land while Washington is asking allies to keep backing the campaign around Hormuz.

Sources: UN News · NPR · NBC News

06

Tusk tells parliament Russia is preparing strikes it will call accidents

Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk told the Sejm on Thursday that Russia is planning hybrid strikes with drones or rockets against European countries supporting Ukraine, Poland among them, and will present them as accidental in order to "paralyse or at least weaken the motivation" of NATO members to come to the defence of whichever state is hit. The warning follows drone strikes near the Poland–Ukraine border, one of which hit a train station shortly after senior officials had passed through it. Tusk said he would discuss drone defences with President Volodymyr Zelensky and further sanctions with partners including Slovakia. Zelensky said Ukrainian forces had struck eight Russian infrastructure targets supporting the war effort in the previous 24 hours.

Why it matters: Tusk is describing an attack designed to fall below the threshold of Article 5 — deniable enough that allies argue about it instead of responding. Saying so in advance is an attempt to remove the deniability before it is used.

Sources: Al Jazeera · Washington Post · AP via ABC News

Business, Economy & Markets

07

Thursday's session: cheaper oil buys the bond market a day off

Wall Street took back most of the week's losses. The S&P 500 rose 85.95 points, or 1.1%, to 7,637.76; the Nasdaq Composite gained 439.87, or 1.7%, to 26,418.30; the Dow added 316.14, or 0.6%, to 51,778.04; the Russell 2000 rose 0.6% to 2,874.63. The move came from the bond market: the 10-year Treasury yield, which had closed above 5% on Wednesday for the first time since 2007, fell more than 7bp to 4.93%, snapping an eight-session climb. Brent lost about 1% to $104.82 a barrel. Gold rose 2.1% to roughly $4,347 an ounce and bitcoin firmed 0.9% to near $76,500, both helped by the softer dollar and lower yields. Europe was subdued by comparison, the DAX up 0.53% at 25,538.

Why it matters: Equities did not rally because the Fed turned dovish — it did the opposite. They rallied because oil fell and took the inflation premium in long-dated yields with it. Which confirms that the barrel, not the committee, is setting the discount rate right now.

Sources: AP via WTOP · CNBC · Yahoo Finance · BabyPips

08

Saudi Arabia says the pipeline will be back, and crude comes off its highs

Oil fell for a second session after Riyadh indicated it can restore roughly half the capacity of the damaged East–West pipeline within days and full operation within about six weeks. Saudi Aramco is also offering extra cargoes to Asian refiners through ship-to-ship transfers near Oman, an improvised route around the disruption at the Yanbu terminal. Brent has now retreated from the $107-plus it reached earlier in the week, when the closure of the pipeline — the workaround for the contested Strait of Hormuz — left the kingdom with no clean way out to the Red Sea.

Why it matters: Europe's inflation path, and therefore the ECB's and the Bank of England's next decisions, currently runs through a single Saudi pipeline and a ship-to-ship transfer zone off Oman. Six weeks of repairs is the most consequential number in the energy market this morning.

Sources: CNBC · Trading Economics

Technology

09

King Charles convenes the AI industry and asks it for principles

The King met senior figures from Nvidia, OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic at Dumfries House in Ayrshire on Thursday, the headquarters of his King's Foundation, in a closed session on what he called the "fundamental principles" that should guide the technology. Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang, Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis, OpenAI chief financial officer Sarah Friar and the UK's AI minister Kanishka Narayan attended. Charles said AI's advance was "intriguing and deeply concerning in equal measure" and that "those in our world who value our humanity and its vital moral component are anxiously seeking your reassurance that we will not lose control of our destiny", warning of the existential danger of such tools falling into the wrong hands. The palace framed the meeting as advancing thinking on principles rather than producing a text.

Why it matters: Britain has spent three years trying to make itself the venue where AI governance gets discussed, and has largely lost that role to Brussels and Washington. Using the monarchy, which binds no one and offends no one, is the remaining instrument — and it says something that convening power is what the UK has left to offer.

Sources: NBC News · UPI · AP via Washington Times

Science

10

A blood test detects early pancreatic cancer that the standard marker misses

Nature Medicine published a prospective study across four countries, covering 1,785 people, of a test combining a ten-microRNA signature read from blood exosomes with the existing CA19-9 marker. In external testing the combined score, PANXEON, distinguished pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma or high-grade dysplasia from controls with an AUROC of 91.7%; for stage I–II disease sensitivity was 86.8%, with specificity of 96.8% among average-risk controls and 84.4% among high-risk ones. The microRNA signature still separated early cancers from controls in participants whose CA19-9 was below the clinical cutoff, suggesting the two markers read different biology.

Why it matters: Pancreatic cancer is usually found too late to operate, and CA19-9 alone is not good enough to screen with. This is a diagnostic-accuracy study, not a screening trial — it shows the test can separate known cases from known controls, not yet that using it saves lives — but it is the first result in a while with the numbers to justify that next trial.

Sources: Nature Medicine · AACR · OncLive

Automotive

11

The EU asks China to cap hybrids after the BEV tariffs pushed exports into hybrids

Brussels is asking Beijing to limit sales of China-built hybrids to around 15% of the EU market voluntarily, or face higher tariffs, according to the Financial Times. Chinese hybrid imports have risen more than tenfold since the EU imposed duties on battery-electric vehicles — from about 3,800 cars in October 2024 to roughly 50,000 in July 2026 — as manufacturers rerouted around a tariff that only covered pure EVs. BYD, the main beneficiary, is building out local production in response: an adviser said the company will need three assembly plants and a battery factory in Europe over the longer term, on top of the €4bn Szeged plant in Hungary that starts assembling the Dolphin Surf in the fourth quarter of this year.

Why it matters: This is the second attempt to solve the same problem, and it shows the first one failed: a tariff drawn around a technology, rather than around an origin, simply moved the trade into the adjacent category. Whatever Brussels agrees now sets the terms on which Chinese carmakers become European manufacturers.

Sources: CnEVPost · electrive · CnEVPost · Automotive World

The Federal Reserve's G.17 release is the first hard activity reading published since Wednesday's hike, and the first clean look at what a year of $100-plus crude has done to American factory output.

Why it matters: The committee has just told markets it will keep tightening into an energy shock. Evidence that output is already buckling is what would force the 16 dots pointing at another hike to move.

15

Pristina, a third day after the verdict

Protests outside the government building and the EULEX headquarters ran on Wednesday and Thursday. Kurti has said the judgment should be corrected on appeal; the defence teams have a limited window to file, and Thaçi still faces a separate obstruction-of-justice case before the same court.

Why it matters: Kosovo's government is simultaneously the target of the anger and the body responsible for protecting the EU mission the crowds are marching on. How it handles a third day determines whether this stays a protest or becomes a governing crisis.
