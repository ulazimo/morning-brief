The War's Inflation Bill Arrives in the Bond Market

Top Stories This Morning

01

Iran hits Gulf states for a second night; Washington answers with sanctions rather than bombs

Iran fired drones and missiles at Kuwait and Bahrain for a second consecutive night. Kuwait's army said it intercepted the attack; Iran's military claimed a hit on Ahmad al-Jaber Air Base, and a US official said no facility hosting American forces was damaged. Rather than strike back immediately, the administration said it would move to choke Iran's economy, with Energy Secretary Chris Wright promising "absolutely maximum pressure" through sanctions on Iran's remaining trade partners. Separately, Tehran accused the United States of a war crime after a missile strike hit a wedding, killing four people — two women and two children — and wounding more than 60; US Central Command said it was looking into the reports, and Vice President JD Vance said he was "extremely sceptical" of the claim.

Why it matters: Trump had said a further Iranian response would bring a much larger American attack. Choosing sanctions instead is the first sign in a week that Washington wants an off-ramp — but the attacks are now landing on Gulf states, which means the war has widened even as the retaliation slows.

Sources: France 24 · RTÉ · CBS News · Just Security

02

Government borrowing costs hit multi-decade highs across the rich world

The sell-off that began with the oil shock has become the defining trade of the week. Japan's 10-year yield crossed 3% for the first time in three decades and held there; the German 10-year Bund reached 3.38%, its highest since 2011; UK 10-year gilts touched a post-2008 high of 5.25%; and the US 10-year Treasury hit 4.81%, its highest since November 2023, before easing back to about 4.78% on Thursday. The 30-year US yield is above 5.2%. Analysts point to three forces at once: heavy government issuance, an energy-price shock that has revived inflation, and the growing expectation that central banks stay tight for longer.

Why it matters: This is the war repricing the cost of money everywhere. Long yields set mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs and what governments pay to run deficits — and unlike an oil spike, a re-rating of long-term inflation expectations does not unwind when the shooting stops.

Sources: CNBC · CNN Business · Bloomberg

Victims' associations and Sarajevo have condemned it; the practical effect is to put Srebrenica back at the centre of two election campaigns at once.

04

Serbia's snap election settles on 25 October — with the decree still unsigned

Public broadcaster RTS reported that the early parliamentary election will be held on 25 October, the later of the two dates Vučić floated on 20 August, and the Republic Electoral Commission met on Thursday evening to begin preparations. The formal steps have still not happened: the government must propose dissolution of the National Assembly and the president must sign the decree, and the legal deadline for doing so if the vote is to happen before the end of October falls on 10 September. Under Serbian law the election must be held 45 to 60 days after it is called. The student-led movement that has driven almost two years of protests says it will not name its candidates for the 250-seat parliament until the date is formally set.

Why it matters: Until the decree is signed, nothing is binding — and the movement's decision to withhold its list until then means the shape of the opposition's challenge, the single biggest unknown in the campaign, stays hidden for up to another week.

06

Malta acquits Yorgen Fenech of ordering Daphne Caruana Galizia's murder

Eight of nine jurors voted to clear the businessman of complicity in murder and of criminal association over the 2017 car bombing that killed the investigative journalist outside her home. Fenech was arrested on his yacht in 2019 after a middleman was given a pardon to name those involved; the men who planted and detonated the bomb have been convicted, but nearly nine years on, no one has been convicted of ordering the killing. Caruana Galizia's family called it a terrible decision. The Committee to Protect Journalists said the absence of a convicted mastermind sets a damaging precedent well beyond Malta.

Why it matters: The murder brought down a Maltese government and became the reference case for the EU's anti-SLAPP and media-freedom legislation. An acquittal at the top of the chain is the outcome that tells anyone contemplating the same thing that the commissioning of a journalist's killing can go unpunished inside the European Union.

Thursday's session: one dovish Fed voice lifts everything except Broadcom

Fed Governor Christopher Waller said a rate rise would not be warranted if underlying inflation keeps slowing — a direct counterweight to Chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish signal a week ago, with markets pricing roughly a two-thirds chance of a hike this month. Yields came off their highs and stocks rallied: the S&P 500 rose 1.06% to 7,747.71, the Nasdaq Composite gained 1.4% to 26,584.06, and the Dow added 624 points, or 1.18%, to 53,686.11. The exception was Broadcom, which fell about 6% despite tripling AI chip revenue to $16.7bn, up 221% year on year: fourth-quarter guidance of $34.8bn came in just under consensus and gross margin is being diluted as custom accelerators and high-bandwidth memory take a larger share of the mix. Gold rebounded about 2% to roughly $4,480 an ounce as the dollar and yields retreated; WTI settled up 0.3% at $91.30 and Brent slipped to $95.52; bitcoin was flat near $77,300.

Why it matters: The rally was bought on one governor's sentence, not on data. Broadcom is the tell in the other direction — the AI trade now punishes companies that deliver enormous growth at thinner margins, which is what the whole custom-silicon build-out is starting to look like.

Technology

09

The US government tells a federal court that training AI on copyrighted work is fair use

The Justice Department filed a statement of interest in the Southern District of New York urging the court to hold that using copyrighted text to train large language models is fair use, arguing that a contrary ruling would distort copyright law, suppress innovation and weaken US competitiveness and national security. The filing addresses The New York Times's claims against OpenAI directly and says its reasoning applies to the parallel suits brought by book authors and publishers. It is the first time the federal government has formally taken a side in the wave of AI copyright litigation. A statement of interest carries no binding authority. The Times responded that the administration "is siding with a handful of trillion-dollar AI companies at the expense of the countless American creators whose work they stole."

Why it matters: The government is not a neutral bystander here: OpenAI has been in discussions since July about giving Washington an equity stake of around 5%. Whatever the court does with the filing, it signals that US policy on the central legal question hanging over the industry is now to resolve it in the labs' favour.

Science

10

The Arctic melt season is a week longer than in the 1980s, and autumn is the reason

A review of nearly 50 years of satellite records, led by Melinda Webster of the University of Washington's Applied Physics Laboratory and published in Nature Reviews Earth & Environment, finds the pan-Arctic melt season has lengthened by about 7.5 days since 1979. Almost all of that comes from freeze-up starting later in autumn rather than melt starting earlier in spring: open water absorbs heat through the summer and the ocean has to shed it before ice can form and survive. Thinner snow cover, smoother and thinner ice and more open water reinforce each other, and the paper notes the changes are disrupting habitats and food webs, not only ice extent.

Why it matters: Headline sea-ice minimums get the attention, but it is the delayed autumn freeze that determines how thick the following winter's ice is — which is the variable that decides whether the trend continues. Separate work published this year finds the lengthening has plateaued since about 2010 into a highly variable regime, so the direction is clear while the year-to-year path is not.

Sources: University of Washington · Phys.org · Communications Earth & Environment

11

France begins road-testing Tesla's Full Self-Driving before an EU-wide vote

Transport Minister Philippe Tabarot said France has started testing two Tesla vehicles fitted with FSD on French roads, a reversal from July when he said the safety trade-offs — particularly on speed limiting and driver-attention warnings — did not yet justify authorisation. Paris wants to verify data supplied by Tesla and by the Dutch road authority RDW, which granted provisional approval in April, prompting Belgium, Denmark, Estonia and Lithuania to follow. Results are expected in the second half of September, ahead of a bloc-wide vote that could come as soon as October. Separately, Polestar cut its 2026 delivery guidance to 61,900–63,100 cars after the US barred the China-linked manufacturer from selling model-year 2027 vehicles.

Why it matters: France has been the loudest sceptic, so its tests effectively decide whether the EU approves a driver-assistance system that has never been validated under European type-approval rules. Approval would open the bloc's largest single regulatory market to the software Tesla's valuation rests on.

Sources: Reuters via The Star · TechXplore · Reuters via Investing.com
