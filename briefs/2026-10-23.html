The AI trade is being repriced in every time zone, and oil is rising into it.

Meta spent more on data centres last quarter than its entire business brought in, and the number did what a year of warnings could not: the Nasdaq had its worst day since June, chip stocks fell nearly 7%, and Tokyo and Seoul opened lower again this morning. Brent closed at a five-month high after Washington ordered embassy families out of Saudi Arabia. Serbia’s campaign ended last night with two rallies across town from each other and a vote on Sunday nobody expects to be conceded quietly. Russia hit Kyiv with ballistic missiles hours after agreeing to new talks. France banned protests in four cities and people marched anyway. Battery-electric cars have now outsold petrol in the EU for the year. The World Series opens tonight in Milwaukee.

Top Stories This Morning

01

Meta burns more cash than it makes, and the AI trade finally cracks

Meta’s third quarter was strong on the top line — revenue of $64.60 billion, up 26% — and that was not the number that mattered. Capital spending reached $34.9 billion, roughly $2 billion more than the whole business generated in cash, leaving free cash flow at negative $2.1 billion, the company’s first cash outflow in more than a decade. It raised the floor of its 2026 capex range to $135–145 billion and disclosed a $9.89 billion charge to settle teen mental-health litigation brought by state attorneys general. The shares fell nearly 7%.

The selling spread well beyond Meta. The Nasdaq Composite dropped 2.6% to 26,008.86, its worst session since June; the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 6.7%, with Lam Research down almost 12% on supply-constrained guidance, AMD down more than 8%, Micron 6% and Nvidia 4%. CoreWeave, whose credit-default swaps have been elevated for weeks, fell 10%. Nvidia’s Jensen Huang, speaking in Berlin, said demand from AI cloud providers was “off the charts” but that capital was scarce and holding growth back — a remark traders read as confirmation that the build-out is now a financing story. Asia followed overnight: the Nikkei and Kospi were both lower again this morning, and China’s CSI 300 is 20% below its June peak.

Why it matters: For two years the market treated AI capital spending as an asset being built; Meta’s cash flow statement reframes it as debt being taken on, and the hardest-hit names were the ones that borrow to buy chips rather than the ones that sell them.

Sources: CNBC on Meta · Investing.com · NBC News · Investing.com on Asia

02

Washington pulls embassy families out of Saudi Arabia; Brent closes at a five-month high

The State Department ordered the departure of non-emergency US government employees and their families from the embassy in Riyadh and its consulates, citing escalating Houthi attacks on Saudi territory. Spokesperson Tommy Pigott said Iranian-backed Houthis had increasingly struck civilian airports and critical infrastructure; recent attacks include a barrage on King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh and debris from an intercepted drone over the Shaybah oil field that killed two people, one of them an Indian national. Aramco says production was not interrupted. Pigott denied wire reports of a wider Gulf drawdown, which AFP had extended to the UAE, Jordan, Qatar and Bahrain.

Brent settled up 4% at $113.17 a barrel, its highest since 4 May, and WTI rose 2.4% to $99.47. The move also reflected a CNBC report that US and Israeli planners have drawn up options for renewed strikes on Iran after the 3 November midterms — planning only, with no approval given, and Donald Trump has repeatedly declined to authorise a return to major combat this year.

Why it matters: Ordering families out is the step governments take when they expect to be shot at, and markets price it that way; with Hormuz already mined, a second front against Saudi export infrastructure is what turns a shipping-risk premium into a supply problem.

Sources: Newsweek · Reuters via Malay Mail · CNBC · CNBC on oil

03

Serbia’s campaign ends with two rallies across town from each other

The student-led movement closed its campaign on Thursday with a rally that filled Slavija Square and the streets around it — reported as tens of thousands by some outlets and hundreds of thousands by others — while Aleksandar Vučić held his own closing event in a sports hall elsewhere in Belgrade and predicted his list would win by several hundred thousand votes. Vučić resigned the presidency last month to run for prime minister; polls put the Students’ List and his Serbia Unified list close to level, with most smaller parties in danger of missing the 3% threshold for the 250-seat parliament. The EU and international observers have raised concerns about near-total state control of broadcast media, and Reuters reported this week on an activist detained for two days by masked officers. Vučić’s party denies accusations of repression and corruption. Separately, Republican Congressman Joe Wilson has asked Secretary of State Marco Rubio to sanction four businessmen close to the government, among them the owner of Pink TV.

Why it matters: This is the first vote in Vučić’s 14 years in power where the opposition is a street movement rather than a party, and both sides have now publicly set expectations of winning — which makes Sunday night a dispute over the count rather than a concession.

Sources: Al Jazeera · AP via Washington Post · Reuters via US News · RFE/RL

Serbia & the Balkans

04

Two acquittals in Belgrade cast doubt on sending Bosnian war crimes cases to Serbia

Serbian courts have now acquitted both Željko Novaković and Milenko Karlica over the killing of 24 Bosniak civilians at Alići and Ekići in June 1992. The cases were transferred from Bosnia to Serbia in early 2022 under a bilateral cooperation protocol; Novaković’s acquittal was upheld on appeal in August, and the Humanitarian Law Centre reported Karlica’s this month. Marijana Cestić Jelovac of the HLC said the prosecution submitted no new evidence and called the verdict problematic. Murat Tahirović, of the Association of Victims and Witnesses of Genocide, said any case sent to Serbia “will not get its necessary epilogue.” For comparison, a Bosnian court in 2024 sentenced ten Bosnian Serb servicemen to a combined 162 years for crimes in the same Bosanski Novi area.

Why it matters: The transfer protocol exists because many suspects hold Serbian citizenship and cannot be extradited, so if Belgrade will not convict, the practical effect of cooperation is that the cases end rather than move — and Sarajevo loses its reason to keep sending them.

Sources: Balkan Insight / BIRN · Genocide Watch

05

Russia hits Kyiv with ballistic missiles hours after agreeing to new talks

Vladimir Putin told US envoy Steve Witkoff in St Petersburg that Moscow would return to trilateral talks next month. Within hours Russia fired three waves at Kyiv; Volodymyr Zelensky counted 21 ballistic missiles, said some carried cluster warheads, and Ukraine’s air force said none of the ballistic missiles were intercepted. At least two people were killed and more than 30 injured in the capital and surrounding region. Ukraine sent drones the other way: Moscow’s mayor said 630 were flown toward the region overnight, with two people hurt and most intercepted. No date has been set for talks, and there is no sign Russia has moved off its maximalist terms — the rest of Donbas, a much smaller Ukrainian army, and no NATO membership.

Why it matters: The pattern of agreeing to talk and then striking the capital the same night is now established enough to read as policy rather than coincidence: Moscow is negotiating about the terms of a settlement while demonstrating it does not need one.

Sources: NBC News · Irish Times · Reuters via Business Standard

06

Macron says “illegitimate” protests will be banned; four cities ban Thursday’s marches

After a week in which cost-of-living protests across France turned into rioting and looting, Emmanuel Macron said demonstrations judged illegitimate would be prohibited in the coming days, blaming ultra-left groups and vandals. Prefects in Paris, Lyon, Lille and Grenoble banned Thursday’s marches and night curfews were imposed in parts of Paris and other cities; union-organised marches were exempted, though they can be rerouted. Thousands marched in Paris on Friday regardless, with organisers saying a court had accepted their request to lift the ban. Student groups say they will keep mobilising through the autumn school break, and larger demonstrations are planned for Saturday. Macron has also pushed back at foreign leaders commenting on the unrest, calling some remarks “as unacceptable as they are stupid.”

Why it matters: France is six months from a presidential election in which Marine Le Pen is running on order, so every banned march that goes ahead anyway is an argument the government cannot control the street — which is the argument she needs.

Sources: NBC News · SBS · Euronews

Thursday was not a broad rout. The S&P 500 fell 1.21% to 7,663.43, its worst day since July, and the Nasdaq 2.6% to 26,008.86, but the Dow rose about 12 points to finish near 52,868 — roughly flat, which means money leaving semiconductors mostly stayed in the market. The ten-year Treasury yield dropped more than 17 basis points to 4.97%, below 5% for the first time since 23 September, as money moved into bonds. Gold fell about 2.5% to roughly $4,040 an ounce — unusual alongside a tech selloff and lower yields, and consistent with positioning ahead of the Federal Reserve’s 27–28 October meeting with headline CPI at 3.7%. Tesla, reporting Wednesday evening, beat on adjusted earnings at $0.48 a share against $0.45 expected, on revenue of $28.05 billion that was flat on the year; GAAP net income fell to $787 million from $1.37 billion, operating income dropped 56% on AI and R&D spending, and the company cut planned 2026 capital spending to $20 billion from $25 billion. Deliveries of 486,532 were down 2.1% but well above consensus.

Why it matters: A falling yield and a flat Dow on a day the chip index drops 6.7% is rotation, not panic — but Tesla cutting capex by $5 billion while Meta raises its floor tells you the two companies now disagree about whether this spending cycle pays.

08

EU energy ministers will discuss bloc-wide demand cuts next week

Ministers meeting on 29 October will take up a proposal, raised by Ireland as Council presidency, for coordinated EU-wide reductions in energy consumption, on the assumption that disruption through the Strait of Hormuz could last months rather than weeks. The Council paper does not specify cuts or commit anyone to them. A separate Commission proposal to aggregate energy demand at EU level and allocate it to member states by need is also with capitals. G7 countries have already agreed a coordinated release of around 100 million barrels over four months, and Austria, Spain, Italy and Croatia have cut fuel excise duties temporarily.

Why it matters: Europe spent 2022 learning to ration gas; this is the first time since then that mandatory demand reduction has been put on a ministerial agenda, and it is being prepared before winter rather than during it.

Sources: Euronews · European Commission

Science

10

Astronauts’ hearts handled six months in orbit well enough for Mars

A study in Circulation by researchers at Northwestern Medicine, UT Southwestern and UT Houston followed 13 astronauts through roughly six-month stays on the International Space Station, with crew members running specialised echocardiography on themselves to track cardiac volume, blood flow and muscle strain. Heart function dipped early in flight and then adapted, and on return the astronauts showed no major cardiovascular strain when tested under simulated Martian gravity. All followed NASA’s exercise countermeasures programme: up to two hours a day, split between endurance and resistance work. Lead author James Thomas said the results give reason to think a crew could reach Mars and work on the surface. The sample is small and the Mars gravity was simulated on Earth, not experienced.

Why it matters: Cardiac deconditioning has been one of the standing physiological objections to a multi-year Mars mission; this does not remove it, but it moves the burden of proof toward bone, radiation and the psychological load instead.

Sources: Northwestern Now · Medical Xpress · Universe Today

11

Battery-electric cars have outsold petrol in the EU this year

ACEA’s September figures, published Thursday, put battery-electric vehicles at 22.7% of EU new-car registrations for January to September — 1,931,465 cars — against 21.2% for petrol, down from 27.7% a year earlier. Hybrids remain the largest single category at 36.3%, with plug-in hybrids at 10.1%. September alone was stronger still: 290,117 battery-electric registrations, up 72.9% year on year, for a 29.9% monthly share. Total EU registrations rose 5.7% over the nine months. The policy backdrop is moving the other way: the Commission’s December 2025 proposal would replace the 2035 zero-emission requirement with a 90% CO2 cut, keeping plug-in hybrids and range extenders on sale, and Emmanuel Macron and Friedrich Merz agreed a joint line on 16 October to soften the rules further as Parliament and Council work on the file.

Why it matters: Europe is loosening the 2035 mandate in the same quarter that the market it was meant to force arrived on its own — and the carmakers lobbying hardest for the delay are the ones losing European share to Chinese brands that build electric cars cheaply.

Sources: ACEA · Electrive · Clean Energy Wire
