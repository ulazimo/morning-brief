The Strait of Hormuz Sets Europe's Inflation Rate

Top Stories This Morning

01

US strikes Iran again after mines are found in Hormuz

President Donald Trump said on Tuesday that the United States had carried out "large and powerful" strikes on Iranian targets near the Strait of Hormuz, in retaliation for what he called a failed Iranian attempt to lay sea mines in the waterway and for a ballistic missile launch at a US base in Jordan. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said their missiles hit US drone hangars at Prince Hassan Air Base; Jordan said it intercepted 10 of 13 missiles that entered its airspace, and Kuwait and Bahrain reported hostile drones over their territory. Trump warned that a further Iranian response would be met "at a much harder and higher level" and that "if it goes a third time, they're going to be totally wiped out as a country".

Why it matters: The memorandum of understanding the two sides signed in June was meant to wind the war down; instead the fighting has settled onto the chokepoint that carries a fifth of the world's seaborne oil, and each round now prices straight into fuel, freight and inflation everywhere else.

Sources: NBC News · CBS News · The National · RFE/RL

02

Euro area inflation jumps to 3.3%, its highest of the year

Eurostat's flash estimate put annual inflation in the euro area at 3.3% in August, up from 2.9% in July. Almost all of the increase came from energy, which rose 14.3% year on year against 10.3% a month earlier, as oil and gas prices climbed on the Iran war and the disruption to shipping through Hormuz. Services inflation actually eased, to 3.0% from 3.3%. Among the large economies Spain was the outlier at 4.5%, with Italy at 3.2%, Germany 2.9% and France 2.7%.

Why it matters: The reading is more than a point above the European Central Bank's 2% target and is being driven by exactly the kind of supply shock a central bank is normally told to look through — except that this one has no obvious end date, and it arrives while the Federal Reserve is openly weighing a rate rise.

Sources: Eurostat · Euronews

03

Germany blames Russian intelligence for the Leipzig airport drone

Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt said on Tuesday that the explosive-laden drone found inside the secure zone at Leipzig/Halle airport on 4 August — close to a Ukrainian cargo aircraft, its detonator removed by a bomb-disposal robot — was operated by agents working on behalf of Russian state entities, and that investigators have proof. Berlin is closing a Russian consulate in response. Leipzig/Halle is one of Europe's largest freight hubs and hosts NATO's Strategic Airlift International Solution, which flies almost daily to the alliance's eastern flank.

Why it matters: Berlin has attributed arson and sabotage to Russia before, but this is an attempted bombing on German soil aimed at NATO's airlift for Ukraine — a clear step up the hybrid-warfare ladder, and one that forces a response Germany has so far avoided.

Sources: CNN · Euronews · Al Jazeera

Serbia & the Balkans

04

Bosnia's campaign opens this week, with Dodik off the ballot

Bosnia and Herzegovina votes on 4 October for the tripartite state presidency, the state parliament and both entity assemblies, including the 83-seat National Assembly and the presidency of Republika Srpska. The official campaign period begins on Friday. Milorad Dodik cannot stand: he was stripped of the Republika Srpska presidency in 2025 after a conviction for defying the international High Representative, and banned from office for six years. His party ran Siniša Karan in the resulting early election and won it narrowly, with a rerun in 17 municipalities in February confirming the result.

Why it matters: This is the first regular general election since Bosnia's international overseers removed the dominant figure in Republika Srpska, and the clearest test yet of whether that removal weakened his party's machine or simply gave it a grievance to run on.

Sources: Balkan Insight · France 24 · Balkan Insight

05

Serbia still has no election date, and no body to bury

Twelve days after saying the snap parliamentary election would fall on either 18 or 25 October and that the date would be confirmed "in a few days", Aleksandar Vučić has not named one, and parliament has not been dissolved. In parallel, Belgrade is still waiting for the tribunal in The Hague to release the body of Ratko Mladić, who died in UN custody on 27 August; Justice Minister Nenad Vujić has said the government will organise a funeral with the highest military and state honours, with the burial site left to the family. Vučić has called the court's refusal to let Mladić die in Serbia "uncivilised behaviour, without precedent or justification". The residual mechanism has opened a standard inquiry into the death.

Why it matters: An 18 October vote now leaves barely six weeks of formal campaign, and the government would be running it while staging state honours for a man convicted of genocide — a combination that will define how Brussels reads Serbia's accession file this autumn.

Sources: European Western Balkans · Balkan Insight · Reuters via US News

World

06

Nepal–China flood toll passes 1,000 with 4,400 still missing

A week after a glacier collapse high in the Himalaya sent rock, ice and meltwater down the valleys on both sides of the border, Nepal's disaster authority reported 1,010 dead, 1,473 injured, 11,814 rescued and 3,916 missing; Chinese state television put Tibet's toll at 16 dead and 546 missing. Together that is more than 1,000 confirmed dead and over 4,400 unaccounted for. The initial rockfall was violent enough to register as a magnitude 5.2 seismic event. Among the missing are 583 foreign nationals from 38 countries, and one review has since concluded that warning signs were visible before the collapse.

Why it matters: This is now among the deadliest glacial-outburst disasters on record, and the failure mode — an unstable glacier above valleys filled with new roads, villages and hydropower sites — is one that warming makes steadily more likely across the range.

Sources: Al Jazeera · CNN · weather.com

Business, Economy & Markets

07

Tuesday's session: oil up, everything else down

Wall Street fell on the renewed US–Iran exchange and firming rate-hike bets. The S&P 500 lost 0.71% to 7,631.47, the Nasdaq Composite 1.03% to 26,099.77 and the Dow 419 points, or 0.79%, to 52,766.88, with the selling concentrated in megacap technology. Europe was weaker still in places: the STOXX 600 slipped 0.56% to 647.46 and Germany's DAX fell 1.02% to about 25,989. Brent traded around $94 a barrel, roughly $25 above a year ago. Treasury yields hit a 19-month high, the 10-year at 4.80% and the 30-year at 5.26%, while the two-year sat at 4.40%. Gold fell to about $4,369 an ounce, some 22% below its January peak; the euro eased to $1.1593; bitcoin traded near $78,000 and ether near $2,450.

The driver is a single repricing. After Fed chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech, futures moved from roughly a one-in-three chance of a September rate rise to about 60%, and the oil-driven inflation impulse has hardened it.

Why it matters: Stocks, bonds and gold all fell together, which is what happens when the market marks up the price of money rather than the odds of recession — the usual portfolio hedges stop working at exactly the same time.

Sources: CNBC · Forbes · Bloomberg · Yahoo Finance · Trading Economics · Fortune

Technology

08

The EU decides ChatGPT is a search engine

The European Commission designated ChatGPT a Very Large Online Search Engine under the Digital Services Act on 31 August — the first AI chatbot to be classified this way — alongside Reddit and Roblox as Very Large Online Platforms. OpenAI had reported about 159.1 million average monthly recipients for ChatGPT search in the EU, far above the 45 million threshold; the Commission treats the product as a hybrid service that qualifies because it answers user queries partly by searching the web. The three services have four months to comply with systemic-risk assessments, annual independent audits, data sharing with regulators and data access for vetted researchers.

Why it matters: Regulators have chosen to govern AI assistants as information gatekeepers under existing platform law rather than wait for the AI Act's heavier obligations, which puts chatbots on the same footing as Google Search for questions of ranking, manipulation and researcher access.

Sources: European Commission · Euronews

09

Anthropic signs a $35bn cloud deal with Nvidia-backed Lambda

Anthropic has agreed to buy roughly $35bn of computing capacity from Lambda, which will install Nvidia hardware at a data centre of about 350 megawatts being developed by Hut 8 in Nueces County, Texas. Nvidia holds the lease on the site, having struck its own agreement with Hut 8 weeks earlier. Hut 8 expects initial energisation in the first quarter of 2027 and the first Phase 2 data hall in the second quarter of 2028. The deal follows commitments of roughly $45bn with Nscale, $50bn with Fluidstack and $45bn with SpaceX.

Why it matters: AI compute is now being contracted at the scale of national infrastructure programmes, and increasingly in a circle — the chipmaker leases the building, finances the operator and supplies the silicon its customers then rent, which makes the sector's true capital exposure hard to read from any one balance sheet.

Sources: Bloomberg · Hut 8 filing

Science

10

A "quantum bath" entangles distant qubits with no active control

Physicists at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria have entangled two superconducting qubits by coupling them to a shared reservoir of correlated microwave photons, rather than by measuring and correcting them in real time as current machines do. The engineered environment pushes the separated qubits into an entangled state and holds them there, confirming a theoretical prediction made more than two decades ago. The work, by A. Andrés-Juanes and colleagues with P. Rabl and J. M. Fink, is published in Physical Review X.

Why it matters: Linking separate quantum processors is currently the hard part of scaling them, because the usual method needs fast, low-noise feedback on every link; a system that stabilises entanglement passively removes a control problem rather than optimising it.

Sources: ISTA · ScienceDaily

Automotive

11

BYD's exports more than double as Volkswagen drops out of the BEV top ten

BYD sold 440,293 new-energy vehicles in August, up 17.8% year on year, with exports of 189,466 units — a monthly record and a rise of 134.5% from a year earlier. Overseas shipments now account for well over a third of its volume. In the same global ranking of battery-electric sales, Volkswagen fell out of the top ten, while China's Leapmotor reached third place for the first time. Chinese brands are forecast to sell about 1.3 million EVs in Western Europe this year, a 10.3% share, against 740,000 and 6.3% last year.

Why it matters: Europe's EV market is growing — battery-electric cars were 22.2% of sales in the first half — but the growth is being captured by imports, so strong European EV numbers no longer imply healthy European carmakers.

Sources: CnEVPost · Car News China · Forbes

A record £2.36bn Premier League window closes

The English window shut at 23:00 BST on Tuesday, with the other big European leagues closing the same evening. Premier League clubs spent a record £2.36bn over the summer, including about £255m on deadline day itself. Chelsea were the biggest spenders, ahead of Tottenham and Manchester City, which is rebuilding in its first season after Pep Guardiola. Nottingham Forest, 16th last season, signed seven players on the final day. Clubs that filed deal sheets before the deadline had a two-hour grace period to complete paperwork.

Why it matters: Squads are now fixed until January, so the shape of the title and relegation races is set — and a record outlay in a season when broadcast growth has flattened puts more clubs closer to the profit and sustainability limits.

Sources: Premier League · Flashscore
