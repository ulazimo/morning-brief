A Twenty-Six Point Far-Right Landslide, and Nobody Who Can Form a Government

Top Stories This Morning

01

AfD wins Saxony-Anhalt with 43.8% — and still may not govern

Preliminary official results give the Alternative for Germany 43.8% in Sunday's state election in Saxony-Anhalt, more than two and a half times the 17.2% taken by Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democrats, who have supplied the state's minister-president since 2002. It is by far the strongest result the party has recorded anywhere. Lead candidate Ulrich Siegmund said the AfD had "made history in this state." But the party fell short of an absolute majority of seats, and every other party in the Landtag maintains its refusal to govern with it, so who actually forms an administration in Magdeburg is unresolved. Merz made no statement on Sunday evening.

Why it matters: The "firewall" has until now been a workable rule because the AfD polled in the twenties and thirties. At 43.8% it produces a parliament in which the largest party is excluded and the remainder may not add up to a majority — which is how a cordon sanitaire stops being a defence of the system and starts being a demonstration that the system cannot produce a government. With two more eastern states voting before the next federal election, that argument is the AfD's most valuable asset.

Sources: France 24 · NPR · Al Jazeera · NBC News

02

The Gulf war becomes a tanker war: US destroys three Iranian ships, Tehran plans a Hormuz "exclusion zone"

After the Revolutionary Guard fired ballistic missiles at a US aircraft carrier and a guided-missile destroyer on Saturday — both evaded, no American casualties — CENTCOM struck three IRGC-linked crude carriers: the M/T Downy off Kharg Island, the M/T Stark 1 near Jask, and the unladen M/T Kylo, destroyed in the Gulf of Oman. Admiral Brad Cooper put the logic plainly: "If you shoot at two of our ships, we will impose an even higher economic cost — taking out three of yours." On Sunday Mohsen Rezaei, the new secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, said Tehran would declare an exclusion zone running from the US blockade line through the Strait of Hormuz and into the Gulf, and blacklist any vessel identified inside it. The US Navy says more than 20 warships now support the blockade, which has turned back 92 commercial ships and disabled three.

Why it matters: Both sides have stopped trying to hit each other's forces and started trying to close each other's shipping. That is a slower war but a far broader one: it puts every neutral tanker owner and marine insurer in the Gulf on one side of a line or the other, and it is why Brent finished last week above $95 after an 8% gain.

Sources: US Central Command · NPR · Fortune · PBS NewsHour

03

Vučić's party names him as its candidate for prime minister

The Serbian Progressive Party formally proposed on Saturday that Aleksandar Vučić become prime minister if it wins the early parliamentary election expected on 25 October. Vučić told party officials he will call the vote in the coming days and then resign the presidency in order to campaign. Parliament has still not been dissolved; the legal deadline for the government's proposal and the presidential decree is 10 September if the country is to vote before the end of October.

Why it matters: In June, under pressure from almost two years of student-led protests, Vučić promised to step down. What is now on the table is a move from a largely ceremonial presidency to the office that actually runs the government, with a loyalist expected to take the presidency. The concession the protests won turns out to be a reshuffle of which chair he sits in.

Sources: AP via WTOP · ABC News · The Washington Post

Serbia & the Balkans

04

Mladić commemorated in Belgrade without state honours; Brussels cancels a visit

Hundreds filed past Ratko Mladić's coffin at the Army House in Belgrade on Saturday, among them Serbia's justice minister and the president of Republika Srpska. The military honours trailed earlier in the week did not materialise: there was no artillery salute and no ceremonial military presence. EU enlargement commissioner Marta Kos cancelled a planned visit to Serbia in protest, calling the glorification incompatible with the values underpinning Serbia's accession path; Vučić dismissed her criticism as "stupid, as usual." Mladić, convicted of genocide at Srebrenica, is to be buried at Topčider cemetery today.

Why it matters: Belgrade stepped back from formal state honours but not from the commemoration itself, and the price is a visible rupture with the commissioner who runs Serbia's EU file — three weeks before Bosnia votes on 4 October.

Sources: Euronews · Balkan Insight · RFE/RL

World

05

Witkoff and Kushner shuttle Moscow to Kyiv; a 72-hour pause, no breakthrough

Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Vladimir Putin in Moscow on Saturday and Volodymyr Zelenskyy in Kyiv on Sunday, the first visit of its kind in months. Both governments held off striking each other's capitals to allow the trips, with Putin ordering a 72-hour pause on strikes against Kyiv from midnight on 6 September; there is no wider ceasefire, and Russia and Ukraine exchanged strikes elsewhere throughout. The envoys called the Kyiv talks "substantive" and Witkoff spoke of a "good resolution," with Washington pushing for trilateral talks. Zelenskyy was blunter: "We expect the war will continue," and said Ukraine is counting on US energy and air-defence support through the winter.

Why it matters: A pause negotiated to protect two visiting envoys is not a peace process. But it is the first time in months that Washington has put senior people in both capitals inside 48 hours, and it comes while American munitions and attention are committed to the Gulf — which is the main reason Moscow can talk and escalate at the same time.

Sources: NPR · NBC News · TIME · The Kyiv Independent

06

Friday's session: a blowout jobs number puts a September rate hike back on the table

August payrolls came in at 162,000 against a consensus near 55,000, with unemployment steady at 4.1%. Stocks gave back most of Thursday's Waller-driven rally: the S&P 500 fell 0.38% to 7,718.60, the Nasdaq Composite 0.29% to 26,506.99, and the Dow 271.86 points, or 0.51%, to 53,414.25. The 10-year Treasury yield rose about 3 basis points to 4.79% and the 2-year hit its highest since January 2025. Futures now put roughly 60% odds on a quarter-point increase at the 15–16 September meeting, and UBS moved to forecasting two hikes, September and December. Europe held up better: the STOXX 600 added 0.2% to about 650, with Volkswagen up 6.6% on its restructuring plan and ASML up more than 3%. Brent settled near $95.73, up more than 8% on the week; gold traded around $4,500; bitcoin held above $80,000 after its largest ETF inflow in nine months. Over the weekend Trump publicly turned up the pressure on Fed chair Kevin Warsh not to raise rates.

Why it matters: A labour market adding three times the expected number of jobs removes the argument that the Fed can look through an energy-driven inflation spike. The tension worth watching is not the equity move — it is a chair appointed by this president being publicly told not to hike, two months before midterms, with the market pricing that he will.

Sources: TheStreet · CNBC · Yahoo Finance · Yahoo Finance (crypto)

07

OPEC+ holds October output flat and defers everything else to 4 October

The eight-country core group — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria and Oman among them — met on Sunday and kept October quotas at September levels, an overall ceiling of about 31.01 million barrels a day. September's 188,000 b/d increase had completed the rollback of the 2023 voluntary cuts, and the statement said nothing about November: before deciding, the group must audit members' actual production capacity to set the 2027 baselines that quotas are built on. The next meeting is 4 October.

Why it matters: With American ships disabling Iranian tankers and Iran threatening to blacklist traffic through Hormuz, OPEC+ has chosen not to add barrels into a market whose main problem is that supply cannot physically move. Holding steady is a bet that the spare capacity is worth more later — which leaves the oil price hostage to the Gulf rather than to the cartel.

Sources: CNBC · Reuters via MarketScreener · OPEC

Technology

08

Nvidia buys Hugging Face for about $13bn

Nvidia confirmed an agreement to acquire the open-model platform Hugging Face: roughly $11.9bn to shareholders plus up to about $1bn in equity retention for staff joining Nvidia. It is the company's second-largest acquisition after last year's $20bn purchase of Groq's assets, and it is expected to close in the first half of 2027 subject to regulatory approval. Hugging Face hosts more than three million models and is used by around 18 million developers and 200,000 companies; Jensen Huang said it will continue to support open-source and open-weight models. Its chief executive told CNBC the company approached Huang weeks before the deal.

Why it matters: Hugging Face is the neutral distribution layer for open-weight AI — the place models from every lab are published, compared and pulled from. Buying it puts the dominant supplier of AI hardware in control of where the software that runs on it is discovered. That is the kind of vertical position competition regulators in Brussels and Washington have spent three years saying they would look at.

Sources: NVIDIA · SEC Form 8-K · TechCrunch · CNBC

09

OpenAI ships GPT-6 Astra, with its cyber capabilities deliberately gated

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra to trusted partners on 3 September and to paying users the following day, calling it the "world's most intelligent and aligned" model and claiming state-of-the-art results on computer use, browsing, software engineering, science and professional work. The released version refuses certain categories of prompt, notably in cybersecurity, and the launch announcement devoted unusual space to what the model could do if misused; the company had delayed the release to add safeguards. Pricing is $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output, with a one-million-token context window.

Why it matters: The interesting part is not the benchmark table, it is that a frontier lab shipped a model with a capability deliberately withheld from the public version and said so. That is the first commercial precedent for capability gating rather than post-hoc content filtering — and it will be the reference point in every regulatory argument about what "responsible release" means.

Sources: Al Jazeera · 9to5Mac

Science

10

BepiColombo sheds its engine and begins the hardest part of getting to Mercury

ESA confirmed that the Mercury Transfer Module has separated from BepiColombo's two science orbiters, tracked by deep-space antennas in Spain and Argentina. The module has provided the ion propulsion for eight years of cruising; a power fault earlier in the mission forced engineers to redesign the arrival sequence around its reduced thrust. The remaining stack is due to enter Mercury orbit on 21 November, after which ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter and JAXA's Mio separate on 9–10 December. Science operations are planned to begin in April 2027.

Why it matters: Mercury is one of the hardest places in the solar system to reach — the Sun's gravity means a spacecraft has to shed speed rather than gain it — and this will be the first mission to put two orbiters around it at once, measuring the planet and its magnetosphere simultaneously. Everything from here is irreversible: there is no propulsion module left to correct a bad approach.

Sources: ESA · Sci.News

Automotive

11

Volkswagen approves 50,000 more job cuts — 100,000 in total by 2030

VW's supervisory board unanimously backed chief executive Oliver Blume's "Future Plan" at a meeting in Wolfsburg, doubling the reductions agreed since late 2024 to roughly 100,000 positions, or about 8% of the group's workforce with this tranche alone. The plan cuts the model line-up by as much as half by 2035, explores alternative uses for four plants, targets a 9% operating margin by 2030 and commits €135bn of investment through 2031. The company called it the biggest overhaul in its 89-year history. Shares rose 6.6% on Friday.

Why it matters: Europe's largest manufacturer is not restructuring for a downturn; it is resizing permanently for a market with fewer models, lower German demand and Chinese competitors it cannot match on cost. The share price reaction tells you investors think it is overdue — and the IG Metall negotiation over how 100,000 jobs actually disappear is now the largest single industrial-relations question in Germany.

Sources: CNBC · Transport Topics · The Spokesman-Review

12

Tesla's Cybercab hits the street in Austin and draws a federal probe the same day

NHTSA opened an Audit Query into Tesla's self-certification that the Cybercab meets all Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards, hours after roughly 1,000 of the vehicles began commercial service in Austin. The Cybercab has no steering wheel, no brake or accelerator pedal and no mirrors permanently fitted. The agency will examine the technical data and processes Tesla relied on to certify a vehicle built without conventional manual controls.

Why it matters: US carmakers self-certify compliance; the regulator checks afterwards. Tesla has tested whether that system permits a vehicle lacking the controls many of the standards are written around, and NHTSA has said it will find out. A similar audit query held Zoox up for four years — and the deployment is already running while the question is open.

Sources: NHTSA · TechCrunch · The Detroit News

13

Antonelli wins at Monza from the back of the grid

Kimi Antonelli took a back-of-the-grid penalty for exceeding his power-unit allocation, then drove through the field to win the Italian Grand Prix, passing team-mate George Russell on fresh tyres with three laps left for a Mercedes one-two. Max Verstappen finished third. He is the first Italian to win at Monza since Ludovico Scarfiotti in 1966 and now leads the drivers' championship by 66 points. At the US Open, Carlos Alcaraz beat Tommy Paul 6-4, 6-3, 6-4 to reach the quarter-finals, where he meets Ben Shelton; Frances Tiafoe knocked out Daniil Medvedev in straight sets, and Aryna Sabalenka faces Linda Nosková in the last eight.

Why it matters: A penalty at Monza was supposed to be the one thing that could reopen the title fight. Instead it produced the largest championship lead of the season, and with a home win at the circuit that mattered most to him, the fight is effectively over barring reliability.

16

Coalition arithmetic in Magdeburg — and Merz's answer

With the AfD on 43.8% and the CDU on 17.2%, the remaining parties must establish today whether any combination of them reaches a majority in the Saxony-Anhalt Landtag, and the chancellor has yet to say anything publicly about the result.

Why it matters: If the arithmetic fails, Germany faces the choice it has avoided for a decade: a minority government dependent on far-right tolerance, fresh elections, or the end of the firewall. Merz's first sentence on this will define his party's position going into 2027.
