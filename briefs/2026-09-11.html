The Oil Shock Becomes a Rates Shock

Top Stories This Morning

01

The ECB raises rates again, and Lagarde calls it a "no brainer"

The European Central Bank lifted its deposit rate by 25 basis points to 2.50% on Thursday, its second increase since the US–Iran war began, with the main refinancing rate at 2.65%. The bank held its 2026 inflation forecast at 3.0% but raised 2027 to 2.5% and 2028 to 2.1%, while upgrading growth to 0.9% this year on a euro-area economy that has proved more resilient than expected. Christine Lagarde said the decision was a "no brainer" and declined to rule out another move.

Why it matters: A central bank that spent two years cutting is now tightening into an energy shock it cannot control, and traders responded by pricing in more hikes rather than fewer — the clearest sign yet that the oil spike has stopped being a commodity story and become a monetary one.

Sources: Euronews · Bloomberg · FXStreet

02

Government bond yields close on 5% worldwide ahead of today's US inflation print

The US 10-year Treasury yield has climbed about 18 basis points this week to roughly 4.96%, its highest since 2023 and within reach of levels last seen in 2007. It is a global move: Germany's 10-year Bund is at its highest since 2011, UK gilts at a post-2008 peak and Japan's 10-year above 3%. The drivers are stacked — Brent above $100, heavy government issuance, record corporate borrowing (AI companies alone account for more than $1.5tn of new debt this year) and the expectation that central banks now stay tight for longer.

Why it matters: The risk-free rate is the price of everything else; at 5% the arithmetic changes for mortgages, corporate refinancing and the equity valuations that have carried the AI trade.

Sources: Bloomberg · Trading Economics · CNBC

03

Vučić dissolves Serbia's parliament; the election is set for 25 October

The Serbian president signed two decrees on Wednesday, dissolving the National Assembly and calling an early parliamentary election for 25 October — 46 days out, and more than a year ahead of schedule. He justified the timing by citing social tensions and the need to protect preparations for Expo 2027 in Belgrade. Vučić has said he will resign the presidency and stand as his party's candidate for prime minister if the Serbian Progressive Party wins.

Why it matters: After nearly two years of protests triggered by the Novi Sad canopy collapse, this is the first national test of Vučić's rule — on ground and a timetable he chose himself, and with his power base potentially moving from the presidency to the office that actually governs.

Sources: ANSA · Al Jazeera · Hans India

Serbia & the Balkans

04

The opposition enters the campaign in two columns

Signature collection for candidate lists began on 10 September, and anti-government voters face a split ballot. One column is the citizens' group "Studentska lista — Studenti pobeđuju", founded this week out of the student movement that has driven the protests; the other is "Evropska Srbija", a broader bloc built around the Freedom and Justice Party, SRCE and Solidarity, with figures including Dragan Đilas, Marinika Tepić and Zdravko Ponoš. Boris Tadić's Social Democratic Party and the Movement of Free Citizens have stood aside and endorsed the students, and a group of academics has published an open letter urging the remaining opposition parties to do the same.

Why it matters: Serbia's threshold and D'Hondt allocation punish fragmentation; whether these two lists merge, coordinate or simply compete is the single biggest variable in the 25 October result.

Sources: Mašina · Moj Novi Sad · Portal Analitika

World

05

Ukraine's drones reach Russia's Arctic gas heartland, 3,000 km from the border

Ukraine's Special Operations Forces said drones flew more than 3,000 km to strike the Novy Urengoy and Purovsky condensate plants in the Yamalo-Nenets region on 9 September — the deepest attack inside Russia of the war. Both are Novatek facilities; Purovsky processed 13.4mn tonnes of gas condensate in 2025. Russian officials confirmed a fire at an industrial energy site but attributed it to falling debris and reported no casualties. The damage to output is not yet clear. Separately, Moscow launched 149 drones at Ukraine overnight into Thursday, 128 of them intercepted, while Volodymyr Zelenskyy was in Canada securing an air-defence package from Mark Carney.

Why it matters: Siberia was the part of Russia's energy complex assumed to be out of range; if that assumption is gone, so is the cost calculation behind Moscow's refusal to move on terms in the US-brokered talks.

Sources: Al Jazeera · Ukrainska Pravda · Euronews

06

British police open a criminal investigation into Reform UK's funding

The Metropolitan Police's special enquiry team is investigating whether Reform UK broke the ban on foreign political donations, after an undercover Channel 4 News report appeared to show senior figures discussing how a fake US donor could route money to the party, including £30,000 for polling and a possible £500,000 payment. Head of policy James Orr and senior adviser Dan Jukes have stepped down. The allegations fall under the Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act 2000, and the inquiry has been folded into an existing probe of other Reform donations.

Why it matters: Reform leads most UK polls; a criminal case over foreign money is the first serious legal threat to a party that expects to contest the next general election as a favourite.

Sources: CNN · AP via Local 10 · Novara Media

07

Britain's settlement trade ban takes hold as Israel closes the UK consulate

The UK has banned imports of all goods from Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank and barred British firms from providing services to them — financing, construction, infrastructure, real estate and advertising — with the measures phasing in over six to nine months. Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband said the government's official position is that the occupation is unlawful. Israel responded by announcing the closure of the British consulate in Jerusalem, with Foreign Minister Gideon Saar accusing London of working "systematically" against Israel. France and Canada have said they will take similar steps.

Why it matters: It is the first time a G7 state has applied a broad economic boundary between Israel proper and the settlements, and it turns a legal position held for decades into enforceable trade law.

Sources: Al Jazeera · NBC News

Business, Economy & Markets

08

Thursday's session: a fourth straight fall, with wholesale inflation doing the damage

US wholesale prices set the tone. August PPI rose 0.4% on the month and 5.4% over the year — the highest annual reading of 2026, up from 4.8% in July and a tenth above forecast — with energy up 4.2% and diesel alone up 24.1%, accounting for more than a third of the goods increase. Further up the pipeline, processed intermediate goods were 11.5% dearer than a year ago.

Equities fell for a fourth consecutive day. The Dow lost 316.56 points, or 0.6%, to 52,064.10; the S&P 500 fell 0.58% to 7,591.70; the Nasdaq Composite dropped 0.65% to 26,081.72. Europe took it harder after the ECB: the STOXX 600 shed 1.4%, the DAX 1.7% to 25,361 and the FTSE 100 1.3%. Brent held above $100 after closing at $101.21 on Wednesday, its highest since May, and WTI topped $100. The 10-year Treasury ended near 4.84% before pushing toward 4.96% overnight. Gold fell about 1.7% to near $4,330 as real yields and the dollar rose; bitcoin has been trading around $81,000.

Why it matters: Traders now put roughly 60% odds on a 25bp Fed hike on 16 September. August CPI, out at 08:30 ET today and expected at 0.4% on the month and 3.4% on the year, is the last input before that meeting.

Sources: BLS · Quartz · Motley Fool · ABC News

Technology

09

The Justice Department probes whether Nvidia structured its Groq deal to dodge merger review

The DOJ has sent Nvidia a formal request for information over the $20bn arrangement it struck with AI chip startup Groq in December — a non-exclusive licence to Groq's technology combined with the hiring of founder Jonathan Ross and several executives, but no purchase of the company. Antitrust investigators are examining whether the structure was designed to avoid the reporting requirement that applies to conventional mergers. Fines are the likely remedy if wrongdoing is found; unwinding the deal is considered improbable.

Why it matters: Licence-plus-acquihire has become the standard way the largest AI firms absorb smaller ones without regulatory scrutiny; this is the first serious test of whether that structure holds.

Sources: Bloomberg · Axios · DigiTimes

10

Apple ships a foldable and the first 2nm phone chip

At Wednesday's "Surprise and shine" event Apple introduced the iPhone Duo, its first foldable, with a 7.6-inch inner display, a 5.4-inch cover screen and Apple Pencil support — a first for an iPhone. It starts at $1,999 and arrives on 23 October. The iPhone 18 Pro ($1,199) and Pro Max ($1,299) and the Duo all run the A20 Pro, the first smartphone processor built on a 2nm process, with two "desktop-class" cores Apple says are 20% faster and a redesigned 7-core GPU up to 40% quicker. The standard iPhone 18, 18e and Air 2 slip to spring 2027.

Why it matters: Two structural firsts in one launch: Apple abandons the single annual iPhone cycle it has run for a decade, and 2nm silicon reaches consumer volume — a manufacturing milestone as much as a product one.

Sources: MacRumors · 9to5Mac · Macworld

11

DeepSeek hires CITIC for a Shanghai listing

The Chinese AI lab has retained four underwriters led by CITIC Securities to prepare an IPO on Shanghai's STAR Market, alongside a pre-listing funding round that would value it at about ¥500bn (roughly $75bn). No prospectus, timetable or price has been published, and the Wall Street Journal has reported a 2027 listing; the money is earmarked for compute, model development and retaining staff.

Why it matters: China's frontier labs have been funded privately and opaquely; a domestic public listing would give one of them permanent access to capital markets at a moment when compute, not talent, is the binding constraint.

Sources: Seoul Economic Daily · Silicon UK

Science

12

August was the joint-hottest month ever measured

The EU's Copernicus Climate Change Service reported on Thursday that August 2026 averaged 16.96°C across land and ocean — 0.85°C above the 1991–2020 baseline and 1.65°C above pre-industrial levels, tying July 2023 for the warmest month in the record. It is the first month since November 2025 above the 1.5°C mark. Sea surface temperatures outside the polar regions averaged 21.07°C, an August record, beating 2023. Western Europe broke its 2003 heat benchmark.

Why it matters: A single month above 1.5°C does not breach the Paris target, which is measured over decades — but the ocean record matters more than the air record, because that heat is released back to the atmosphere over years.

Sources: Copernicus · CBC · Eunews

Automotive

13

Washington tells Ford to cut its China ties; Ford refuses

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy wrote to Ford chief executive Jim Farley urging the company to wind down its battery and vehicle arrangements with CATL, Geely and BYD, citing national security and CATL's place on a Pentagon list of firms alleged to have military links. At issue is Ford's use of licensed CATL technology at its Marshall, Michigan battery plant. Farley called the letter a "wrongheaded attempt to capture headlines", said it contained basic misunderstandings, and noted that Marshall is wholly owned and run by Ford and that Geely-built vehicles are for Europe, not the US.

Why it matters: Every Western carmaker's affordable-EV plan runs through Chinese battery chemistry and licensing; if the US treats those licences as a security problem rather than a commercial one, the cost floor for American EVs moves up.

Sources: Detroit News · Quartz · Autoblog
