The IMF named the AI trade a systemic risk, and the memory chips obliged

The Fund published its financial stability report yesterday with an AI equity correction at the top of the risk list, on the same day a Chinese memory maker’s product plan knocked roughly $100bn off Micron, SK hynix and SanDisk and dragged the Kospi down 5.5%. The Vatican said Pope Leo XIV has lung cancer and has had part of a lung removed. Iran’s campaign against shipping pushed Hormuz traffic to its lowest since July, and Britain blamed the Revolutionary Guard for a plot against an American air base in Gloucestershire. Ukraine got its first night without a strike on the grid since Trump’s ceasefire announcement — and five ballistic missiles on Kyiv instead. Serbia votes in eleven days. September US inflation lands at 8:30 Eastern.

Top Stories This Morning

01

Pope Leo XIV has lung cancer; surgeons removed the lower lobe of his right lung

The Vatican said the diagnosis — lung adenocarcinoma — came from a pre-operative examination, and that a roughly 90-minute video-assisted operation at Rome’s Gemelli hospital on Tuesday removed the lower right lobe without complications. Leo, 71, woke normally and returned to the hospital’s tenth floor. The Vatican has not said what stage the cancer is; lymph nodes have been sent for testing, and it said doctors are not currently discussing chemotherapy pending further results. All audiences are cancelled through 20 October. Oncologists quoted before the operation said that if the nodule proved cancerous, an early-stage tumour removed in full carries an excellent prognosis.

Why it matters: A pope eighteen months into his pontificate now has a cancer diagnosis on the record and a recovery that will be measured in weeks, not days — and the Vatican’s silence on staging is the part that will drive the next fortnight of reporting.

Sources: CBS News · NBC News · AP via PBS

02

The IMF put an AI correction on its shortlist of things that could break markets — the same day memory chips lost $100bn

The Fund’s Global Financial Stability Report, out Tuesday, names three “plausible near-term catalysts for a sharp tightening of financial conditions and market turmoil”: a correction in AI-linked equities, a sharp rise in sovereign bond yields, and a major cyberattack. It is not a crash call. The argument is about amplification — concentration (the largest seven US tech companies are now over 35% of S&P 500 market value, against roughly 12% a decade ago), leverage in the chain of deals between hyperscalers and weaker AI firms, and forced selling. Hours earlier the sector gave a live demonstration: after CXMT’s president told a Shanghai conference the Chinese firm expects to ship DDR5 server memory on a denser 4F² cell design around year-end, Micron, SK hynix and SanDisk shed about $108bn in combined market value, Micron falling 4–5%, and South Korea’s Kospi closed down 5.46%. Counterpoint data cited in coverage put CXMT at 10% of the global DRAM market in the second quarter, up from 4% a year earlier.

Why it matters: Memory was the one part of the AI build-out where pricing power looked unassailable, so a credible Chinese entrant at the server end is exactly the kind of single headline the IMF is describing — small news, large repricing, because so much sits in so few names.

Sources: The Register · Quartz · Benzinga · Invezz

03

Hormuz traffic falls to its lowest since July as Iran widens the campaign — to an air base in England

At least a dozen tankers were attacked in and around the Strait of Hormuz in the past week on a tally of UKMTO warnings, six of them on Sunday alone, with oil, LNG and LPG vessels all hit; the Revolutionary Guard claimed another strike on Monday, its third in two days. Kpler counted seven tankers transiting on Tuesday, less than half the seven-day average and the fewest since 23 July. Brent settled at $105.69. In London, Prime Minister Andy Burnham told Parliament that the September incident at RAF Fairford — three vans carrying petrol stopped near the US-run base on 27 September, no explosives found — was “highly likely to have been coordinated in Iran, almost certainly directed by Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard.” Seven men have been arrested, six British nationals and one dual British-Iranian; all are on bail and none charged. Britain has summoned Iran’s ambassador and promised further sanctions. Iran denies involvement. The US had already moved its bombers out of Fairford.

Why it matters: Iran cannot beat the US Navy in the strait, but it can keep insurers and charterers away from it, and the IMF’s growth forecast published the same day simply assumes the waterway reopens by March. The Fairford attribution extends the same logic onto NATO soil three weeks before the US midterms.

Sources: The National · CNBC · NBC News · Washington Post

Serbia & the Balkans

04

Eleven days out, Serbia’s election fight has moved to the Administrative Court and the diaspora ballot

The Republic Electoral Commission rejected the Student List’s objections to how voting abroad will be run — the location of overseas polling stations, and hours set to Serbian time — in a session on 10 October, carried with 11 votes in favour, one abstention and 21 members not voting. The list has filed appeals with the Administrative Court in Belgrade, arguing the commission let the 72-hour decision window lapse and then dismissed the complaints anyway. The underlying numbers are the dispute: registered diaspora voters have more than doubled past 100,000, while polling stations abroad rose only from 81 to 93, and online registration was locked at 23:59 on 3 October. Separately, the monitor CRTA reported that one in three campaign activities by the “Aleksandar Vučić — United Serbia” list that its observers watched on the ground showed elements of manipulation or possible legal violation, including suspected signature forgery, misuse of public office and resources, and offers of money, help or jobs for support. Eighteen lists are on the ballot; the Student List holds its closing rally in Belgrade on 22 October.

Why it matters: In a race two pollsters cannot agree on, tens of thousands of diaspora votes and a contested procedural record are the raw material for disputing the result — which is now a court’s decision rather than a campaign argument.

Sources: Vreme · Danas · Balkan Insight · European Western Balkans

05

Bosnia’s election produced record-low turnout and left Dodik stronger without being on the ballot

Final counting from the 4 October general election confirms turnout about 9.5 points below 2022, around 40% — the lowest since the war — with Željka Cvijanović of Milorad Dodik’s SNSD re-elected to the Serb seat of the state presidency on roughly 55.6%, Denis Bečirović (SDP) taking the Bosniak seat and Darijana Filipović (HDZ) the Croat seat. The SDA leads the state House of Representatives on about 19.6% and nine seats; SNSD has six on about 16.9%. Dodik himself could not stand: he was convicted in February 2025 of defying the international High Representative and barred from office for six years. He campaigned anyway, told his closing rally that Bosnia was “dead” and an “impossible country” with Russia’s ambassador in attendance, and declared victory. In the Bosniak-Croat Federation, parties with a clear pro-Western and pro-Ukrainian line did well.

Why it matters: A six-year political ban has not cost Dodik control of Republika Srpska’s politics, and a 40% turnout gives whoever forms a state government very little authority to move Bosnia’s stalled EU file.

Sources: Balkan Insight · European Western Balkans · AP via PBS

World

06

Ukraine’s grid got one quiet night; Kyiv got five ballistic missiles and no warning

For the first time since Trump announced an “energy ceasefire” on Sunday, Russia struck no Ukrainian energy sites overnight into Tuesday — and hit transport and logistics instead, with Ukraine’s air force counting five ballistic missiles and 108 Shahed-type drones. Five missiles hit civilian infrastructure in Kyiv, killing at least two; Ukrainian officials reported at least ten killed and about 80 injured nationwide over the day, including four dead and 41 wounded in Kharkiv and around twenty hurt by a drone strike on a university in Rivne. Volodymyr Zelensky said Ukraine received no early warning of the Kyiv missiles from its partners, and separately accused Russia of ignoring the ceasefire. Asked whether Russia had violated it, Trump said: “I’ll let you know about that later,” and insisted intelligence sharing continues; Moscow has still not confirmed any agreement exists. Reuters and others report US officials have threatened to curtail intelligence and arms unless Kyiv stops striking Russian refineries. Meanwhile the IAEA agreed with Kyiv in Warsaw to deploy monitors to 19 electrical substations critical to nuclear plant safety — the agency’s largest deployment of its kind.

Why it matters: One night without grid strikes is the first evidence that the announcement is real; a warning that did not arrive is the first evidence of what Washington’s leverage over Kyiv now looks like in practice.

Sources: CNN · Kyiv Post · AP via PBS · IAEA deployment

Brent settled at $105.69, down 0.9%, and the easing in oil let Treasuries rally at the long end, with the 10-year around 5.2%; precious metals weakened as front-end yields and Hormuz risk cancelled out a softer dollar. Bitcoin recovered to about $83,400 after dipping below $81,000 in the Asian selloff, still down roughly 2.8% on the week; ether traded near $2,490. In earnings, Goldman Sachs posted $18.68bn of net revenue and $5.29bn of net earnings, $17.01 a share, on strong equities trading; Citigroup’s profit jumped about 40% to $5.2bn, $2.85 a share against $2.65 expected, with its best third quarter for fixed income in at least a decade; JPMorgan was the exception, at $5.57 a share against roughly $5.90 expected, reported as its first miss in nearly three years. Chief economist Silvana Tenreyro: “The global economy has been resilient, but that resilience is reaching its limits.”

Why it matters: The AI build-out is doing the work in both the market and the forecast — the Fund credits it with offsetting the energy shock, and assumes it moderates rather than snaps. Today’s September CPI is the last inflation reading before the Fed meets on 27–28 October.

Technology

08

Character.AI filed for Chapter 11, with Disney as the $20m lead bidder for its intellectual property

The chatbot company — valued at $1bn at the height of the ChatGPT-era funding wave — filed for bankruptcy protection on Monday and asked the court to approve auction rules making Disney the stalking-horse bidder for a non-exclusive, perpetual licence to certain IP at $20m. Other bidders can top it. Parent Character Technologies listed estimated assets and liabilities of $50m–$100m each. The platform keeps running during the process, which the company expects to conclude in late November or early December, and would be wound down if no buyer commits to operating it. Disney sent Character.AI a cease-and-desist in September 2025 over unlicensed use of its characters, which the company said it removed; Google licensed the firm’s technology and rehired its founders in 2024, and it has since faced lawsuits and state investigations over minors’ use of its companion bots.

Why it matters: The first well-known consumer AI company to go bankrupt is being bought for its character licences, not its models — a price signal that in consumer AI the asset with residual value is the IP, and the distillation-era model weights are worth close to nothing.

Sources: Variety · Reuters via KFGO · Global News

09

Anthropic says small models may be months from self-replicating across cheap smart devices

In research published Monday, Anthropic’s Frontier Red Team argued that if small open-weight models keep improving at their recent rate, they could soon be capable enough to copy themselves onto poorly secured consumer hardware — TV boxes, smart appliances — producing a replicator with no data centre and no central switch to flip. The team ran a small model across fifteen off-brand Android TV boxes costing about $55 each, achieving 0.39 tokens per second, and ran inference on eight of nine hardware types tested. The limits matter: the full replication cycle was demonstrated only on a closed network of emulated devices with a deliberately trivial compromise step, and Anthropic presents its success rates as an upper bound rather than a real-world estimate. The recommendation is hardening the Internet of Things now.

Why it matters: Frontier-model governance has assumed a chokepoint — the data centre — that cheap distilled models running on $55 hardware would remove, which puts the burden on consumer device security rather than on model licensing.

Sources: BleepingComputer · SecurityWeek · Quartz

Science

10

A resupply flight took artificial retinas, lab-grown heart tissue and brain organoids to the space station

SpaceX’s 35th cargo mission for NASA lifted off from Cape Canaveral at 6:33 a.m. Eastern on Tuesday with about 6,300 pounds of cargo, due to dock at the Harmony module at 11 a.m. on Thursday. The science manifest is the point: LambdaVision-1 continues work on manufacturing protein-based artificial retinas in microgravity, where films can be built without the sedimentation that interferes on the ground; Stellar Spheroids grows three-dimensional heart tissue models; and MINDS uses brain organoids to study the inflammation implicated in neurodegenerative disease. The ISS National Laboratory says more than two dozen of its sponsored investigations flew. Dragon is expected to stay until mid-November before splashing down off California.

Why it matters: These are early-stage manufacturing and disease-model experiments, not treatments — but the retina work is one of the clearest tests of whether orbit is a useful factory floor for anything, which is the economic case the station’s commercial successors are being sold on.

Sources: NASA · ISS National Lab · Spaceflight Now

Paris is a show about cheap European EVs, with Chinese brands taking the stands next door

Two days into the Mondial de l’Auto, which runs to 18 October with more than sixty carmakers, the reveals have converged on small and affordable. Citroën’s electric 2CV concept is the headline, aimed at roughly €15,000 and production in 2028; Renault showed the E-Space concept alongside the R5 Turbo 3E and a new Mégane E-Tech; Dacia brought a redesigned, European-built Spring; Smart premiered the #2; Audi showed the A2 e-tron with an LFP pack; Peugeot brought the E-T16 concept and an E-208 GTI; Volvo claims the fastest-charging car at the show in the EX60. Why it matters: Europe’s answer to cheap Chinese EVs is finally a line-up of cheap European EVs — two to three years out, and arriving just as the rule that was supposed to force them is being watered down.
